## Follow one question the whole way through

The clearest way to understand this is to follow one question the whole way through. So let's take a real one — **File output nodes 10**, *what happens when two outputs are aimed at the same name in the same place* — and watch what becomes of it.

## The question is written, and it is written blind

The question starts life in the [Question register](Question%20register.md), which holds every question this project owes, where each came from, and what breaks if the answer comes back wrong.

That last part is why the register is written the way it is. Whoever wrote this question already had a hunch — the Creator has been caught by two outputs colliding in their own work, which is why it is a question at all. **None of that reaches the researcher.** A researcher told what to expect stops looking, and hands back the expectation dressed as a finding. So the question is worded with no hint at the answer, no hint at a mechanism, and no mention of any Blender version. The wording above is the whole of what gets asked.

Two things are worth being exact about, because they are easy to get wrong in opposite directions.

**Naming the thing asked about is not a hint. Naming the answer is.** The question says *file output node*, and it should — that is the thing being asked about, and a question so vague the researcher has to guess what is meant is not blind, it is broken. It makes no difference whether the name is Blender's own or an ordinary industry one; either is fine, and precision beats over-simplifying. What is forbidden is naming what would *settle* it: where to look, which part of Blender holds it, what mechanism to expect, what the answer is likely to be. A question that offers two possible outcomes has done this — the researcher now has two things to confirm rather than an open question to answer.

**On versions the rule is narrower than it sounds.** A question never names a particular version and never says or implies that anything differs between them. It may still speak of versions in general where that is the thing being asked about — what happens when a file made by one Blender is opened by another cannot be asked any other way. Read the rule as *no expectation planted*, not as *the word must not appear*.

**The register is not the question sheet.** The register carries origins and stakes; the sheet carries numbered questions and nothing else. The researcher-runner below prepares the sheet, and having read the register it does no research on that topic itself. This matters most for the questions an agent raised from its own knowledge — those are recorded in the register as exactly the hypothesis a researcher must not be handed.

## It gets a number, and keeps it for good

**File output nodes 10** is that question's name for the life of the project. Numbers are never reused and never renumbered. A question that turns out to be wrong or unanswerable keeps its number with a note saying so, rather than freeing it up.

That is what makes everything downstream cheap. A finding points at a number. A spec points at a number. A comparison lines up four answers by number. Nothing has to be rewritten when the set grows.

## It is asked once for each version, in isolation

Every question is asked separately of every version Smoozy supports, and the answers are never compared until every version has been done.

**In isolation means by an agent that has seen no other version's answers. It does not mean one at a time.** A fresh agent starts cold and remembers nothing, so four running at once are as separate as four running in turn. Where the tool allows it, one chat may therefore start one blind researcher per version and do nothing else itself — the [Researcher brief](../Groundwork/Templates/Researcher.md) carries the wrapper for that, and the [Findings Auditor](../Groundwork/Templates/Findings%20Auditor.md) carries the same for the audit. Where it does not, the versions are run one chat at a time, and the two routes produce the same thing. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-08-02), who asked for it: firing four chats by hand, one after another, was the Creator's own time spent on something no rule required.

Two things bound it. **Only these two steps fan out.** The hand tests, the citation check, the comparison and its check are each one chat over every version, because reading the versions side by side is their whole purpose, and a per-version split would remove the thing they exist to do. And **every version must get identical wording**, differing only in its version and its paths: the moment one is told to search harder than another, a gap in the others is later read as a change in Blender, which is the single most likely error in this stage.

A dispatching chat writes nothing itself. It starts the agents, and each agent writes its own file. What a dispatcher adds to a findings document would be its own words standing where a researcher's should be — and where several agents would write into one shared file, such as a topic's decisions record, none of them writes it at all and the runner copies their lines in afterwards. The versions researched for **behaviour** are the released ones from 4.5 up: **4.5, 5.0, 5.1, 5.2** — at a `#.#` grain, not `#.#.#`. Note what that grain costs: nothing here is published at a finer level — the documentation is written per `#.#`, and Cycles arrives one copy per `#.#` — so a behaviour that changes in a patch release is invisible to all of it, and only a running Blender would show it.

Blender also publishes unstable builds — alpha, beta and release candidates — which working artists commonly use. Those are researched only for **how they are found, named and fetched**, never for how they behave: there is no fixed thing behind them to research. Nothing is said to the user about it, on the Creator's ruling that anyone running an unstable build knows what they are taking on. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-07-30).

One honest limit. A researcher cannot be blind to the *existence* of other versions — release notes are a comparison against the previous version, so a 5.0 researcher meets 4.5 on the first page. What blindness actually protects is that no expectation is planted: the question never says a version, never says anything differs, and no researcher ever sees another version's findings.

## What the researcher is given

A **handout**: the question sheet for one topic, where that version's sources sit, the labels below, and the answer template. Nothing about what the research is for, and nothing about any other version.

**The researcher may read exactly two things — the question sheet, and `Blender Sources/`.** Everything else in this project is barred, `Preparation/` and `Audits/` above all, because those hold this project's own guesses about how Blender behaves. A researcher that reads them hands the guesses back as findings, believing it found them, and nothing downstream can tell the difference. It is the one failure this stage cannot detect.

**That ban is trusted, not enforced, and the Creator chose it knowingly.** Running a researcher in a bare folder outside the project would make it a wall. It would also be one more thing to remember at the start of every single pass, and a rule that must be remembered is a rule that eventually is not — so the risk of forgetting was judged worse than the risk of a leak. Two things follow. The ban is written into the brief **first**, before the task, bluntly and more than once, because that is what an instruction has instead of a wall; whoever fills the brief in must not soften or reorder it. And the researcher must declare in its own answers that it read nothing else, so a leak has to be an outright lie rather than a slip.

Two other things exist because the first attempt failed in exactly this way. `Blender Sources/` sits at the top level rather than inside `Preparation/`, so that "read only your sources" does not put a researcher one folder from the register. And the project's own `CLAUDE.md`, which orders every agent to read `Start Here` first, now carries an exception for researchers — that instruction, faithfully obeyed, is what walked the first researcher straight into the material it was meant never to see.

The handout also does **not** say what Smoozy is. A researcher that knows the app needs a render to resume reads the documentation hopefully. This puts a real bar on the questions — each has to stand on its own with no product context — and it is why the runner shows the sheet to the Creator before any pass begins. There is no separate audit of a question set; that look is the only gate, and it is the cheapest moment to catch a question nobody could answer cold.

One practical note: blender.org refuses plain fetching, so a researcher reading the site needs a real browser.

## Read from a copy, not the website

Blender publishes the manual and the API documentation as downloads. Take them, per version, turn them into plain text, and hand them to the researcher as files. Two reasons, and the second is worth more than the first.

**A copy can be searched before it is read.** Reading on the site means guessing a page, loading the whole of it — menus and footers included — finding it is the wrong page, and guessing again; every miss costs full price. One search across a copy returns the matching lines, and only those get read. The words are identical either way; what changes is how much has to be read to find them.

This matters most for the answer that comes up most. Silence cannot be shown by clicking around, but it can be shown by searching the whole documentation for every word the thing might be called and finding nothing. Where a copy exists, that is how *not in the sources* should be established, and the terms searched for are what the record of looking holds. It turns the weakest kind of answer into the best-evidenced one.

**A copy is frozen.** Blender's documentation changes. Four versions researched over weeks against a moving website is worse ground than one snapshot, and a finding checked months later should meet the words its researcher actually read.

Two rules keep the trail intact. A finding **cites the address, never the file path** — the copy is how it was found, not where it lives — with the date the copy was taken as the date read, recorded once and given to every researcher working from it.

And the way a file becomes an address is **written out exactly, not left to be worked out**. Each source turns differently: the manual and the API documentation swap a `.txt` back for the `.html` they came from, under their own addresses; the release notes drop `.md` and sit under the version with a slash after them. The [Researcher brief](../Groundwork/Templates/Researcher.md) carries all three with a worked example each. Cycles' own files have no address at all, being no kind of web page, and are cited by file and place instead.

That last part is not fussiness. The first researcher ever run on this stage stopped to ask what address a release-notes finding should carry, because nothing said — and a researcher left to guess an address invents one that resolves to nothing, which is a citation that cannot be checked.

The release notes are a copy too, by a different route. Blender's release notes page is built by a script as it loads, so nothing useful can be saved from it — but that page is rendered from plain files Blender keeps in its own developer-documentation repository, one per subject per version: the compositor, Cycles, EEVEE, the core, and about twenty more. Those files are the release notes before a browser gets to them. They are taken the same way, dated the same way, and cited the same way — the address a reader would visit, not the file. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-07-30) that this is the same source, not a new one: the same data, before a browser gets to it.

The split by subject is a small gift: the compositor questions have a compositor file waiting in every version.

**The copies live in `Blender Sources/`** at the top level of the project, one folder per version per source, keeping the structure the download came with. They sit there rather than under `Preparation/` deliberately: a researcher told to read only its sources must not find itself one folder from the register. The contents are kept out of git — a gigabyte of somebody else's documentation does not belong in this project's history, and all of it can be downloaded again. When they were taken is an input the researcher-runner fills into every handout. The manual and the API documentation are turned to plain text once, up front, which is what makes them searchable and drops them from gigabytes to a fraction of that without losing a word. **The manual's pictures are kept**, beside the text and named in it where each one sat: a screenshot of a panel can settle what a thing offers where the words do not, so throwing them away would have quietly narrowed what research can reach. The first set was taken on **2026-07-30**.

## Where an answer may come from

Four documents, and two things that are not documents. Nothing else at all: not the open web, not forums, not question sites, not the source code, not another agent's memory.

Blender's API documentation, Blender's manual, and Blender's release notes — each at the address that names the version being researched. Those addresses exist: `docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.5/` and `docs.blender.org/api/4.5/` both serve that version, and so do the equivalents for the others. There is also an address meaning *the newest*, which is where a search engine usually lands — it must never be what gets cited, because what it serves changes when Blender releases. Observed 2026-07-30: it served 5.2.

**The fourth document is Blender's requirements page, and it is the odd one.** The manual states that minimum and recommended requirements exist and then sends the reader to that page, so without it the heaviest question in *The machine* could not be answered at all. It was taken the same way as everything else and lives in `Blender Sources/requirements/`, named for the date it was taken. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-08-01) that it counts as a source.

What makes it odd is that it has **no version in its address**. It describes whichever release was current on the day it was taken, with a short section naming what particular older releases support. So it speaks for a version **only where it names that version in so many words**, and anywhere else it is describing some other release. Every handout that offers it must say so, and a finding leaning on it for a version it does not name is not carried by it. That limit is not a formality: on the first topic to use it, the page named 4.5 in four lines and none of them was a requirement figure, so the minimums stayed unestablished with the page open.

Because it is dated rather than versioned, a finding cites it with **its own date read**, which is not the date the rest of the sources were taken.

Three more sources sit outside the documentation, and all are **observed** rather than documented — not because they are lesser, but because of how the fact is got. Reading a document, two people find the same sentence. Poking at a running thing, they may not, unless what was done is written down exactly. So an observed finding records what was done and what came back, closely enough that somebody else could repeat it and see the same result.

**Cycles itself**, as the Creator supplies it — taken out of a Blender they have installed, one copy per version. Cycles is an add-on, so what arrives is its own files rather than a sealed program, and they are read directly. This answers a class of question no documentation reaches: every setting Cycles has, under its exact name, with its kind, its default and its range, and how those change from version to version. Where the manual says what a setting is for, this says what it *is*.

Nothing about Cycles makes it a special case in principle — it is simply a thing to be examined rather than a document to be read. It is here because the Creator can supply it and it settles questions nothing else can.

**A warning that goes with it.** What a file *says* and what a running render *does* are not the same claim. Reading that a setting exists is solid ground. Reasoning about what happens when it is used is a step beyond, and that step makes the finding **inferred**, not observed. The line is worth holding: the strongest source in this stage is also the easiest to over-read.

Only what the Creator puts in the folder counts. Going and finding Blender's wider source code is not permitted, and never was — that is hunting, and it lands outside anything anyone has agreed to stand behind.

**A test run on a machine the project can reach.** There are two of those, and every researched version of Blender sits on both and can be opened and tried. This is what a **not in the sources** answer is for — it is the start of a queue, not a dead end. Three rules keep it workable.

**The two machines answer different halves of the question, and are never merged.** The Creator's Mac says what Blender does on macOS on Apple silicon. A rented Linux machine with an NVIDIA graphics card says what Blender does on the kind of computer a Smoozy render actually happens on. Neither speaks for the other, and a finding always says which one it came from. Writing one result as though it covered both would undo the whole point of having two.

**Most of these tests are not the Creator's to run, and treating them as if they were wasted their time for a whole topic.** An agent has a terminal on that same computer, so anything driven from a terminal — starting Blender with no window, typing into its Python console, reading what it prints, checking what it wrote to disk — is the agent's to run. What is genuinely the Creator's is what needs eyes on the window: what a panel says, which boxes are ticked, what a control offers, a screenshot. So a list splits in two before it is handed anywhere, and the Creator only ever sees the second half. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-08-01), after the first topic asked them to paste terminal output by hand.

**From 2026-08-26 the split is gone, and the Creator runs nothing at all.** Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-08-26), in their own words: *"do everything via CLI only, I don't want to do anything by myself."* The half that used to be theirs — what a panel says, which boxes are ticked, what a control offers — does **not** become unanswerable. It moves to a rented Linux machine **with a display server**, where an agent starts Blender with a window from a terminal and photographs it from a terminal. This project has had such a machine before, so this is a machine to ask for rather than a new capability. **What is genuinely lost is narrower than it looks**: only what needs a hand on a mouse in a way no command can stand in for, and each of those is written into the findings as *cannot be run* with its reason, exactly as a missing operating system is. **Do not hand the Creator a list.** A test an agent cannot drive is recorded as unreachable, never passed to them.

Three things hold that split honest. The tests run on **one of the two machines named below**, never on some machine an agent found for itself, and every finding says which one — the rule is that the computer must be one whose exact description is written down here, or *on what kind of computer* stops being true and the finding is worthless. Whoever runs a test writes the machine's **raw output into the record word for word**, exactly as the Creator's own paste would be — an agent that runs a test, sums it up, and then writes up its own summary has removed the only witness. And the agent that runs one is the same one that owns the list and writes the results in, which is self-marking and accepted knowingly: the verbatim output is what stands in for a second pair of eyes, and the citation check reads it afterwards.

**A test that cannot fail is not a test.** Whoever writes one says, in the entry itself, what a negative result would have looked like. If no answer the machine could give would have come out the other way, the test carries no information however carefully it is run and however many times it is repeated. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-08-06), after a question survived a research pass, an audit, a citation check and a comparison check and came back settled-as-unsettled every time, for a reason none of those four named. It asked whether rendering one scene pulls only that scene's references, and the test watched whether Blender *complained* about another scene's missing file. It never did — and "did not complain" and "did not load it" are different things that no number of repeats tells apart. In one version that Blender never names a missing image at all, so the test carried nothing whatever. Every step in this stage asks whether the answer follows from the evidence; none asked whether the evidence could have come out the other way, and this is the line that asks it.

**Compare the picture, not the file.** Blender writes text into every image it saves — eleven separate pieces at factory settings in the newest versions. Seven of them the stamp settings control; four are Cycles' own and nothing controls, and three of those four are times. So two renders of the same frame can differ byte for byte while being the same picture, and whether they match depends on whether those times happen to round alike. A test that compares saved files byte for byte is therefore reading a clock as often as it is reading a render. Compare the pixels. Label: **observed** (2026-08-06), after a claim that two Blender pictures can never match, and a claim that they match with stamping off, were both found wrong.

It is asked **once per topic**, never during a pass — a pass that stops to wait on a person does not finish.

It is **one list covering every version**, grouped by the test rather than by the version. The same test is the same setup whichever Blender it is run in, so an entry says what to build, what to do, what to look at, and which versions to do it in. Whoever runs it opens one version, runs the entry, opens the next, runs the same entry. Asking version by version instead means asking the same thing four times over, and it produced exactly that on the first topic ever run this way.

Grouping is also what keeps the comparison honest. When a test is run in one version and not another, the second version's silence is an untested question, and the comparison reads it as a change in Blender. That is the single most likely error in this stage, and one list run uniformly makes it impossible rather than merely discouraged.

A test settles the operating system it was run on and leaves the others open. Label a hand-run test **observed**, and record which version, what was done, what came back, and on what kind of computer. Creator-stated (2026-07-30) that they are willing to be asked.

## What an answer must contain

Always the label — one of **documented**, **observed**, **inferred**, or **not in the sources** — and always what the answer does not cover. What sits between them depends on where the answer came from, and there are two shapes.

**From a document**, three things: the **exact words** that carry the claim, quoted; **which source** they are in and the full address; and the **date read**. A citation without the words cannot be verified, and a paraphrase is no longer the source speaking.

**A picture in the manual is a document**, and takes the same three things with one substitution: the picture stands where the quoted words would, so name which picture it is and the address of the page it sits on, and say what is **visible** in it. The label is **documented**, because the picture is part of the manual. What is visible in a picture is visible; anything a reader has to supply from it — what a control does, when it appears, why it looks that way — is **inferred** and takes that label instead, like any other step.

That was written into the researcher's brief from the start and was missing here until 2026-08-09, when three auditors in one round each stopped on it and each guessed differently — one relabelled a picture-reading *observed*, another split it, a third left it and asked. Four versions were about to disagree about Blender for a reason that had nothing to do with Blender, which is the one error this stage exists to prevent. The rule has not changed; it now lives where it is looked for.

**From a thing rather than a document**, two shapes, depending on whether the thing can be quoted.

*Where it can be read* — Cycles' own files — quote it exactly, and name which file and where in it. That is as checkable as a documentation quote, and is treated the same way.

*Where it can only be exercised* — a test somebody ran — four things instead: **what was done**, **to what**, **what came back**, and **on what kind of computer**, plus the date. There are no words to quote, so what stands in for them is a description exact enough that somebody else could repeat it and see the same result. An observed finding that cannot be repeated from its own description is as unusable as a quote nobody can find.

**Inferred** earns its own label and is the one to watch. If the quoted words do not say it, and a reader has to supply a step to get there, the answer is not documented. Label it inferred and write the step out. Anything downstream treats an inferred answer as an assumption. Most invented facts are not invented outright — they arrive as a reasonable-sounding step nobody wrote down.

## Not in the sources is an answer

It is expected, and it is the most likely outcome for the behaviour questions, because documentation describes what things are far more often than what they do. Nobody should invent an answer rather than give this one.

It is also the answer with a bar on it, and the bar is the point: it is only accepted with a record of **where the researcher looked** — which pages, which searches, what was ruled out. Without that, nobody can tell a thorough miss from a lazy one, there is nothing for a check to check, and nothing for a hand-run test to be aimed at. An unrecorded *not in the sources* is not an answer, it is a blank.

Downstream, a **not in the sources** finding lands as **unresolved** under Rule 2 — and goes on the list of things the Creator could settle by hand.

## Two things every answer says

Every answer, whatever the question, fills in two standing lines. They are not extra questions — they are one line each, so that neither can be forgotten.

**Driven from outside.** Smoozy never opens Blender's interface: every render it starts, and every file it reads, happens from outside. So each answer says whether it holds when Blender is driven from outside its own interface, and where that differs from inside. An answer that only holds inside the interface is worth nothing here.

**Operating system.** The artist's computer is one kind and a rented machine is another, so files and paths cross between them constantly. Each answer says whether it holds on every operating system, and where it does not.

*Not stated in the sources* is an allowed answer to either, on the same terms as above: only with a record of where it was looked for.

## Where the answer lives

One document per topic per version, under `Findings/`. That shape follows the work: a researcher answers one topic for one version and nothing else, so that is what one sitting produces and what one document holds. An audit takes one of them; the hand-test step and the check open all four; the layout serves both without being organised for either.

**A version may hold more than one, and the second is not a replacement.** When an audit sends questions back to be researched again, the fresh pass writes its own document beside the first — `<topic> — second pass.md` — rather than editing the audited one. Two reasons, and both matter. The new researcher must stay blind, and it cannot be blind while reading the answers it is meant to improve on. And the first document has been audited; overwriting it would throw away work an auditor has already seen and signed off.

What follows is that **every step after that point opens both**. The hand tests, the citation check, the comparison and its check each take a version's documents together, not the newest one. Where the two disagree, that is a disagreement inside one version and is recorded as such — never as a difference between versions. Say so in the handouts; it does not go without saying, and a chat given one path will read one document.

A findings document is unusual in one way, and it is a deliberate exception to the Conventions: its citations sit **beside each finding**, not in a companion sources file. The split exists to keep a document readable for people who need the claim but not the paper trail, and here the paper trail is the entire content.

## The order everything happens in, and why it is that order

A topic goes through seven steps, and the order matters more than any one of them. These numbers are the ones the board uses and the ones the runner uses; there is only one numbering in this stage, and it is this:

1. **Questions** — the sheet is written blind.
2. **Research** — every version answered, one pass each, nothing else in between.
3. **Audit** — each version's answers judged for how the work was done.
4. **Hand tests** — one list, every version, run by the Creator.
5. **Citations** — do the quoted words carry the claims.
6. **Comparison** — the versions laid side by side.
7. **Comparison check** — the comparison read against the findings it rests on.

Three of those placements were got wrong the first time, and the reasons are worth keeping.

**Nothing interrupts the answering.** No checking between passes, no tests to run, no questions for the Creator. A pass that pauses for a person does not finish, and four passes that each pause four times is how one topic ate a week.

**The audit comes before the check.** The check is cheap and mechanical; the audit is the one that sends questions back to be researched again and tests out to be run by hand. Both produce new text. Check first and that new text is unchecked, so the check has to run twice. Audit first and the check runs once, over final words. The old order was the other way round, on the reasoning that auditors would otherwise waste a round on citation errors — but the audit brief tells the auditor not to check citations at all, so the gate bought nothing and cost a second pass.

**The hand tests come after the audit**, because the audit is what discovers which tests were never run, which were run in one version and not another, and which were recorded too thinly to repeat. Gathering the list before that means gathering half of it.

## The audit — one version, one chat

**It is not a round.** A set of findings does not get the four-role loop that a spec gets, and that is settled. What this audit asks — is the label honest, is every question answered, does the finding quote its words, was the search abandoned early — is largely checkable rather than arguable, so there is little spread of judgment to gather from several independent readers, and little for the Creator to rule on. So one model, one Blender version, one chat, doing the whole job: it finds the faults and it fixes them. [Auditing](../Groundwork/Auditing.md) sets this out in full, including what the collapse gives up and what holds instead.

Two things it may not fix itself. Where a finding stopped looking too early, the answer has to come from a **fresh blind researcher**, because this auditor has read the project and can no longer supply a Blender fact. And where nothing but a running Blender will settle it, the item goes on the hand-test list. The auditor writes both down and the runner carries them out.

The Creator says how many passes a version gets. A second pass by a different model lands beside the first.

**What one version alone cannot see** is everything that only shows up across versions: two versions answering the same question in opposite ways, a test run in three and not the fourth, the same reasoning labelled documented here and inferred there. That reading is not lost — it moves to the step below, which opens all the versions side by side anyway.

## The hand tests — one list, one sitting

One chat opens all the versions' findings side by side and does two things nobody has done yet.

**It reads across the versions.** Every place two versions answer the same question differently, every place a test was run in one and not another, every place one version found a sentence another said was not there. This is the reading a single-version audit cannot do, and it is deliberately placed here rather than dropped: what it produces is not a verdict but more entries on the list below.

**It gathers the tests.** Every *not in the sources* answer, every note a researcher left about what a person at a computer could settle, and everything the audits sent this way. Then it packs them — one entry per **test**, not per question and not per version, saying what to set up, what to do, what to look at, which versions to run it in, and which questions in which versions it answers. The same test needed in four versions is one entry, not four.

**Then it splits the list in two, and runs its own half.** Everything reachable from a terminal is the chat's own to run — starting Blender with no window, typing into its Python console, reading what it prints, checking what it wrote. Only what needs eyes on the window goes to the Creator: what a panel says, which boxes are ticked, what a control offers. The rule for sorting an entry is simply whether a terminal can reach it, and the two halves are marked as such in the file so nobody has to work it out twice.

**On the rented Linux machine that split does not apply, and the whole list is the chat's own.** That machine is driven over SSH and has a display of its own, so a chat can open Blender's window on it, drive it, and photograph it with Blender's own screenshot command. Nothing there needs the Creator's eyes, and nothing there should be sent to them. The split above is a fact about not having hands on the Mac, not a rule about what kind of test belongs to whom.

The list is a file in the topic's audit folder, so it survives the chat and the Creator can work through their half over days. As results come back — its own and theirs — the same chat writes them into each version's findings as **observed**, with the machine's raw output word for word rather than a summary of it, plus what was done, to what, on what kind of computer, and the date. It brings the two standing lines up to date where a test settled them, prunes the answered items out of the researchers' own hand-test lists, and adds the decisions-record line that any change to audited work needs.

**The Creator answers however they like, and composing the record is never their job.** This is why the tests are written out in full *before* they are run: the set-up, the steps, the places to check and what to compare all exist on paper already, so a one-line result — "no such control anywhere", "same in both cases" — is a complete answer. Whoever writes it into the findings carries that procedure across in their own words and produces an observed finding that stands on its own. Nobody asks the Creator to describe what they did, and nobody sends a result back as too thin. A thin record is a failure of the list that preceded it, never of the answer. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-07-31).

Five things make a list usable rather than merely correct, and all were gotten wrong on early runs. **Every test carries its own number**, not the number of the question it answers. **Every test names its versions**, on its own line. **Every test says who runs it**, the chat or the Creator. **Anything to be typed is written out in full with its exact location** — the exact terminal command or Python console line — without guessing arguments for undocumented functions; that holds for the chat's own half too, since the words it ran are what the record has to show. Finally, **the Creator is never asked to compare or analyze**; they are only asked to return what they see.

The list must also strictly only ask for what the Creator can actually do. What computers, operating systems, and graphics cards they have is an input the runner fills in, not something to be assumed. A test needing anything else at all (e.g., dual GPUs when the Creator only has an M1 Mac, or a Windows test for a Mac owner) goes in its own section marked "cannot be run" with the reason. Do not put it in the list and hope. The reason is then written into the document that lacks the answer, so the silence is explained rather than bare.

## The check — one chat, every version

Take each claim with its citation, open the source, and rule: supported, partly supported, not supported, not found, or the address is dead. Nothing else counts — not the checker's own knowledge of Blender, and not any argument the finding makes for itself. A finding may explain why its claim is reasonable; that reasoning is not the source speaking, and the checker is told to disregard it. The bar is that the quoted words say it without the reader supplying a step.

This is deliberately outside the audit machinery. "Do these words say this?" has one right answer: there is no spread of judgment to gather from several models, and nothing in it for the Creator to rule on. So it is one agent, every finding, a verdict per finding.

It covers **every version in one sitting**, and it runs late, once the text has stopped moving. Its record goes in the topic's own audit folder under `Citation check/`, one file named for the model that did it — so a second or third check by another model sits beside the first rather than over it. Each entry names the question, the version, the verdict, and, where it disagrees, what the source actually says.

**Where it can reach the machine a test was run on, it repeats the test.** Judging a written procedure repeatable by reading it is a guess, and this is the only check the stage has on a step that marked its own work. It repeats only on a machine written down in the equipment section above, follows the finding's own words without improving them, and where its own run disagrees it records both runs and sends the question back rather than choosing between them. It never writes its result over the first one — it was not in the room for that.

**It fixes what it ruled, in the same sitting.** Not because ruling and fixing are the same job, but because narrowing a claim to what its quote carries means having the source open, and this agent has just read it. The alternative was tried and was worse: it left somebody editing research documents from a summary, without ever seeing the page. There is one hard limit. It may correct a quote, an address or a claim against the page in front of it — that is the same act the researcher performed. It may never answer a question the findings left open; that is a fresh researcher's, always. And it may not repair an observed finding recorded too thinly to repeat, having not been in the room — those go to the hand tests, written out as tests.

## When research and the Creator disagree

**The research wins.** Where a finding contradicts something the Creator has stated about Blender, the finding stands and the Creator-stated version is replaced. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-07-31). This is why the stage exists: the Creator is a Blender professional whose recollection is good enough to plan on, and the whole apparatus of blind questions, sourced answers and hand tests is built to catch the places where good recollection is still wrong.

Whoever finds the clash still **writes it down** — what was stated, what the research found, and which won — in the sources file that held the old version and in that document's decisions record. It is never left for the Creator to remember, and it is never a silent overwrite, because a spec built on the old version may need looking at again.

**Know what this rule costs.** A finding labelled *inferred* is a reader's step from what a source does not say, and it now outranks a professional's direct experience. That is the intended trade — an inference at least shows its working, where a recollection cannot — but where an inferred finding overturns something the Creator stated, say so plainly in the record rather than presenting it as documented fact, and it is worth a hand test if one is cheap.

**First check that it is a clash at all.** The Creator describes Blender as somebody using it; research describes it as the documentation and the property lists have it. The same behaviour told those two ways can look like a disagreement and be none — one output "carrying its own folder" and a folder written inside an output's name are the same thing to the artist doing it. Read for that before recording a clash.

The rule runs one way only. Where something the Creator states contradicts a fact **Smoozy itself decided**, that is not this rule: the agent surfaces both and asks, since a decision is theirs to change.

## The versions are compared

One comparison document per topic, under `Comparison/`, written by an agent of its own once every version has been answered, audited, hand-tested and checked. Comparing findings nobody has checked would spread one bad answer across every version.

It is deliberately not the researcher-runner. That agent wrote the questions and filed the answers; asking it for the conclusion would be asking it to mark its own work.

**It holds the answers and nothing else, and where that line falls is worth being exact about.** A comparison is the one research document downstream reads, so everything in it that is not an answer is noise to the reader it exists for. The test is whether a reader would build something different if a line were not there. What the comparer read, which findings document covers which question, which version had a second pass, what a check corrected and when — none of that changes anything anybody builds, and all of it goes to **`Audits/Blender Research/<topic>/Comparison trail.md`**, which is where the paper about the paper belongs under the Conventions. What stays is the answer, what it does not cover, the label it carries, and which machine it was run on — that last one qualifies the fact rather than recording the work.

Two consequences follow, and both were paid for. Round labels go: a comparison never says a question was added later or compared later, because Rule 6 bans describing the moment of writing and because a reader meeting an answer in question order should not have to work out which round it belongs to. And a disagreement that leaves an answer open still stays — but worded as a fact about Blender rather than about documents. Not *two of this version's documents disagree*; instead *whether this holds is not settled, and the two readings disagree*.

It is built question by question — for **File output nodes 10**, the versions' answers side by side and one line saying which of three things is true:

- **The same everywhere.**
- **It differs**, with what each version does.
- **Not established for every version** — and this third one is the whole reason the comparison needs care. A version answered *documented* beside one answered *not in the sources* is **not** evidence that anything changed. Newer documentation lags. Treating a gap in the documentation as a change in Blender is the single most likely error in this stage, and the comparison must never make it.

**A later round folds into it, and does not pile on top of it.** When a topic gains a question, or a new machine reopens it, the answers go into the questions they belong to and the three lists take their new entries in place. For a while this stage did the opposite — every round was added by pure insertion with nothing deleted, on the sound reasoning that an earlier round's words should not be disturbed. What that produced was documents stratified by date, where the oldest reading of a question sat hundreds of lines above the newest, and where a sentence written for a seven-question round still promised things that the eighth question broke. Insertion protects the record; the record is the findings and the trail, and they are untouched either way. **Where a later round overturns an earlier line, the later reading is what the comparison says**, and the earlier one goes to the trail with the reason.

## And the comparison is checked

The comparison is the one document downstream actually reads, and it is where a whole topic's work can be undone by a single overreaching line. So it gets a check of its own — one chat, one pass, reading each verdict against the four findings it claims to rest on and ruling whether it follows.

Like the citation check, this is a check and not a round: the question is whether a verdict is carried by the findings, which has one right answer. The chat fixes what it can and says what it could not. The one it exists for above all is a silence written down as a difference.

## Where the fact lives afterwards

The fact lives in the finding, and **the comparison owns it** — one document per topic, saying what every version does, question by question, checked against the findings it rests on. Where a fact lives and what anybody reads are two different things, and keeping them apart is what stops a later stage having to open a folder many times its size.

A findings document is the paper trail behind a comparison — many times the length, and saying nothing the comparison has not already said. It is opened by somebody checking the trail, and by nobody else. **Never as part of the ordinary reading**, at any stage. How the comparison itself was built — what was read, which pass answered what, what each check corrected — sits beside it in `Audits/Blender Research/<topic>/Comparison trail.md`, and is opened by the same people and no others.

**But a comparison answers one topic, and a builder's question is rarely one topic's.** What a render's exit status is worth is answered in **four** topics at once. *(Three when this was written; the navigation walk of 2026-08-30 found the door carrying the same stale count and removed it there rather than correcting it, so that it could not go stale again. Here the number is the example's whole point, so it is corrected instead — and if it moves again, say **several** and stop counting.)* A reader who opens one of them and stops gets a true sentence and a false rule — which has already happened here, and reached a spec. That is not a failing of any comparison; it is what a shelf arranged by topic cannot do. So one thin layer sits above them, and it is where every downstream reading begins. The section below sets it out.

So a document that leans on a Blender fact carries its label in the body, as Rule 2 requires, and its companion sources file points at the comparison question that owns it — **File output nodes 10**. Where the versions differ, that entry names the finding for the version in hand as well, so the trail is one step away without being part of the reading. Never a quote of Blender copied into the document.

That is *Every fact has one home* doing real work: when a later version answers differently, one finding changes, the comparison takes a new column, and every document still points at the right place.

## The layer that joins the topics

`Preparation/Blender Research/Across topics/` holds three kinds of document, and there are few of each.

**The map** is the door. It lists the questions a builder actually arrives with, and against each one the joined answer to read first and the comparison questions behind it. **It holds no Blender fact at all** — only the names of questions and what each one asks — so nothing in it can go stale except a question number, and a dead number can be found by searching.

**A joined answer** holds the one thing no comparison can hold: the conclusion that exists only when topics are put together. It says plainly what it does not cover, and what the evidence under it could not have shown whatever Blender did.

**What the research cannot say** is the furthest each kind of condition was ever taken — the largest picture, the most cards, which operating systems, and the rest — and what was never reached at all. It is a fact about the research rather than about Blender, and it is the direct guard against the fault this layer exists for: a *never* drawn from runs that could not have come out any other way.

### What downstream reads

**Design and Architecture begin at the map.** Where an answer spans topics it is already joined, and the joined answer is what is read. A comparison question is opened only where the map, a joined answer, or a document's own sources file names it — that question, and no more of the file than that. `Findings/`, `Questions/`, the [Question register](Question%20register.md), `Coverage.md` and everything under `Audits/` sit outside every downstream reading, at every stage.

**Nobody downstream assembles an answer out of several topics themselves.** That is the whole rule. Everything else here is what makes it possible to obey.

### How this squares with every fact having one home

A comparison owns every fact about Blender. A joined answer owns one thing only — the fact that appears when topics are put together — and that fact has no home in any comparison, so before this layer it had no home at all.

Between the two runs a line, drawn where it can be checked rather than where it reads best.

- A joined answer **carries no quotation of Blender, no message Blender prints, no figure taken out of a message, and no table of what each version does.** Those are the comparison's, and they are what a reader follows a pointer for. A figure that *is* the joined answer — a threshold, a boundary, a count of how many ways something happens — belongs here.
- **Every claim in a joined answer names the comparison questions it rests on.**
- A joined answer **may say in one line what a comparison says in ten**. That is a second copy at a coarser grain, and it is the cost of the layer, paid deliberately: the alternative is a reader assembling the answer themselves, and that reader is the one this stage has already got wrong once.
- **Where the two disagree the comparison wins**, and the joined answer is what gets corrected.

### How it is checked

Every document in the layer is read by an agent that neither planned nor wrote it, against the comparison questions that document names, ruling **both ways**: what does not follow from those questions, and what those questions carry that a builder needs and the document dropped. The record goes in `Audits/Blender Research/Across topics/`, one file per pass named for the model, beside that Subject's decisions record — the same shape a comparison check has. A layer that is nobody's checked work is assertion, however well it reads.

### How it is kept true

A joined answer rests on questions, and questions gain answers. So a round does not close until whoever closes it has opened the map, found every joined answer naming a question in the topics that round touched, brought each up to date and had it checked again. That is one more step at a close. Without it this layer becomes the stalest thing in the stage while reading as the newest, which is worse than not having it.

## Who drives it

One role carries a topic from an empty question sheet to a checked comparison: the **researcher-runner**. It never researches, never audits, never compares and never checks — it hands out briefs, files what comes back, keeps the trackers true, and commits.

Everything above is its memory, not the Creator's. The Creator should never have to know which step comes next, which brief it wants, what the result is called, or where it goes. They choose a model, run a chat, and come back. The runner does the rest and tells them the next thing.

Its steps, per topic:

**Before anything**, read the board — `Audits/Blender Research/Progress.md` — which says where every topic stands and what the next thing is. That is the runner's first act in any chat, including one starting a topic fresh, and updating it is its last act at the end of every step. Then **pick the topic**, unless given one: every topic needs doing eventually, so the only question is order, readable from `Coverage.md` and the register.

Then the seven steps above, which are the runner's steps too:

1. **Write the question sheet** from the [Question register](Question%20register.md), blind, and save it under `Questions/`. Having read the register, it does no research on that topic itself, ever.
2. **A researcher pass per version** — one handover if the tool can start agents, four if it cannot. Hand over a filled-in handout with its containment warning untouched, file what comes back into `Findings/<version>/` with its signatures file beside it, tick `Coverage.md`. Nothing else happens between passes.
3. **An audit per version** — one handover or four, the same way; a model of the Creator's choosing, more than one pass where they want it. Copy each audit's closing line into the topic's decisions record, since none of them writes it. Then carry out what an audit could not do itself: send a question that stopped looking too early back to a **fresh blind researcher**, and put anything needing a running Blender on the hand-test pile.
4. **The hand tests** — hand over the brief, then the list, then the Creator's results.
5. **The citation check** — one chat, every version, which carries out its own rulings.
6 and 7. **The comparison**, then **the comparison check** — two different models.

Then **stop**, and say the topic is finished.

**It marks nothing it made.** It wrote the questions and filed the answers, so the audits, the check, the comparison and the comparison check are all other chats — the same reason a runner never closes its own audit round.

**A dead chat costs nothing.** Every handoff is a file, so if the runner's chat is closed, or the Creator moves to another tool, the next chat reads the board and carries on from the same place. That is the whole reason the board exists.

The Creator's part is choosing models, running each chat, running the hand tests, and saying when a step is good enough.

Seven ready-to-fill briefs sit in `Groundwork/Templates/`: **Researcher Runner** (the one that starts or resumes a topic), **Researcher**, **Findings Auditor**, **Hand Tests**, **Citation Check**, **Comparer**, and **Comparison Check** — plus the ordinary **Auditor**, which this stage does not use.

The Creator starts or resumes a topic by asking for one, in whatever words come naturally, and the agent they ask becomes the researcher-runner and picks the brief up itself. Pasting the brief into a fresh chat does the same thing. [Start Here](../Groundwork/Start%20Here.md) carries that trigger, because the front door is where an agent meets it before anything else.

## When a new version arrives — and when a new question does

Both happen, and the second is the one that gets forgotten.

A **new version** is a new folder under `Findings/`, the same question sheet run again untouched, and a new column in each comparison. Nothing else moves.

A **new question** — a spec grows, an audit raises something — takes the next free number in its topic and then owes an answer from **every version already researched**, including the ones long since finished.

**New equipment is the third, and it reopens finished topics.** Every topic carries a list of tests that could not be run for want of a machine. When a machine arrives that can run them, those lists come back out and the topic goes through steps 4 to 7 again — the tests, a check of what they produced, the comparison brought up to date, and the comparison check. The earlier hand-test list is not edited: a new list sits beside it, named for the machine, exactly as a second research pass sits beside the first. A topic being finished is a statement about the equipment of the day, not about the topic.

`Coverage.md` is the live tracker for both: which questions have an answer from which versions, and which cells are empty. Label: **Creator-stated** (2026-07-30) that adding a future version must stay cheap, for the whole of Preparation and not only for the question set.
