# Add-ons — Blender 4.5, 5.0, 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3 side by side

Where add-ons come from, how they get onto a computer with nobody at a screen, and what a render does with them.

## The machines behind the tests

Five machines carried this topic's tests. The equipment section of `Preparation/Blender Research/Method.md` describes every one of them in full.

- **The Creator's Mac** — a MacBook Pro with an Apple M1 Pro chip, Apple's own 14-core graphics reached through Metal and no second card, 16 GB of memory, with a screen. Every version was tested on it: 4.5.12, 5.0.1, 5.1.2 and 5.2.0 under macOS 26.5.2, and **Blender 5.3.0 Beta, built 2026-10-16, under macOS 26.6.2**. It is the only machine 5.3 ran on.
- **A rented Linux machine with one NVIDIA RTX 4090** and a display server of its own — the only one of this topic's four rented machines that could open a window. 4.5 to 5.2, questions 1 to 11.
- **A rented Linux machine with two RTX 4090s** and no display server. 4.5 to 5.2, question 3.
- **A rented Linux machine with four RTX 4060 Ti** and no display server. 4.5 to 5.2, question 3.
- **A rented Linux machine with one RTX 4080 SUPER** and no display server. 4.5 to 5.2, questions 12, 13 and 14.

In the equipment section those four are the **first, second, third and fourth** rented Linux machines. All four Blenders — 4.5.12, 5.0.1, 5.1.2 and 5.2.0 — sit on each.

**A result belongs to the machine it was got on, and no two are ever merged.** Where the same test was run on more than one, the answer below says so and says whether they agreed; where it was run on one only, the answer says which. **"Both machines" means the Mac and the one-card Linux machine** under questions 1 to 11, and the Mac and the RTX 4080 SUPER machine under questions 12, 13 and 14, **and it speaks of 4.5 to 5.2 only.** Every other machine is named outright.

**For 5.3, nothing ran on Linux, on a machine with no display server, on graphics other than Apple's, with two Blenders running at once, or with a second Blender version.** So a 5.3 answer set beside a 4.5–5.2 answer that only a Linux machine gave is not established, and never a difference.

**The Mac is not one unchanging machine.** Its operating system moved from 26.5.2 to 26.6.2 between the 4.5–5.2 runs and the 5.3 runs, and its own Blender 5.2 from 5.2.0, by way of 5.2.1, to 5.2.2. So a difference between an older Mac reading and a 5.3 Mac reading can belong to the build, the operating system or the day as easily as to the version. **Where 5.3 came out differently from what 5.2 had recorded, Blender 5.2.2 was run beside it on the same Mac the same day.** Where 5.2.2 did what 5.3 did, the difference is not 5.3's; where 5.2.2 did what the 5.2 record said, it is. Each answer below says which.

**5.3 here is a beta build.** Nothing below says the released 5.3 behaves the same.

**Nothing was got by clicking on the one-card Linux machine** — a lock screen covers its display and no click could be shown to work any control — so each panel and menu was opened by one of Blender's own operators, with the identifier read out of Blender first rather than guessed. **Nothing in 5.3 was got by clicking either**: Blender's window was opened from a terminal on the Mac, driven by a script, and photographed with Blender's own screenshot command, so a section drawn closed could not be opened.

**One unevenness matters when reading the window answers.** The five window tests were run on macOS in Blender 5.2 and 5.3, and on Linux in 4.5 to 5.2. So wherever an answer rests on what Blender's window shows, the picture for 4.5 to 5.2 is the Linux one, the Mac speaks for 5.2 and 5.3, and 5.3 has no Linux picture beside it.

**A result settles only the machines it ran on. Windows was not tested at all, in any version.** Every Windows question remains untested, rather than answered or untestable; that is never a difference between versions.

## The answer in short

### Where Blender itself differs between versions

Each question holds the evidence and its limits. **Nothing in this list is a difference between machines.** Every tested 5.3 placing rests on the Mac; the three changes under question 5 also have a same-day 5.2.2 control. The two release-note changes are labelled below.

- **1, 2 and 7 — the real-time engine's name.** `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` in 4.5; `BLENDER_EEVEE` in 5.0 to 5.3. Which name each route accepts is under question 2; this topic did not offer 5.3 the old name, and Images 9 records 5.3 refusing it from a script.
- **1 — what a scene keeps when the add-on that planted its settings is off.** 4.5 still lists `cycles` among the scene's keys; 5.0 to 5.3 list nothing.
- **1 — how many add-ons the workspace add-on filter panel lists.** Eight rows in 4.5, 5.0 and 5.1; three in 5.2 and 5.3. 4.5 to 5.1 were read on Linux only; 5.3 on the Mac only, where it matches 5.2.
- **2 — what `-E help` does after printing the engine list.** 4.5 does not exit normally — it dies on the Mac and hangs on Linux; 5.0 to 5.3 exit 0.
- **3 — the macOS Dock icon when Blender runs in background mode from the command line.** 5.3's release notes say it no longer shows. A change at 5.3 on the release note alone; no test looked at the Dock in any version.
- **3 — a window requested with no display server.** Different refusal streams and endings in 4.5 versus 5.0 to 5.2, with an extra 5.0 warning. Tested on the two-card Linux machine; 5.3 untried.
- **3, 4 and 11 — the starting value of Cycles' `use_hiprt` setting.** False in 4.5 and 5.0, true in 5.1 and 5.2, on two Linux machines. 5.3 is not placed; question 11 explains why.
- **4 and 14 — the second line printed when a package is refused.** Two wordings: 4.5 and 5.0 against 5.1 to 5.3, for platform and declared-version refusals alike.
- **4, 11 and 14 — the Python each Blender carries.** Python 3.11 in 4.5 and 5.0; 3.13 in 5.1 to 5.3. The exact builds and unpacking folders are under question 14.
- **5 — the extension command's subcommands.** Twelve in 4.5 to 5.2; thirteen in 5.3, which adds `repo-info`.
- **5 and 8 — the settings each repository carries.** Fourteen in 4.5 to 5.2; sixteen in 5.3, which adds `show_expanded` and `use_access_token_for_asset_libraries`.
- **5 and 8 — the listing `server-generate` writes for a folder of packages.** 5.3 adds a top-level line, `"assetlib_auth_method": "PER_LIBRARY"`, that 4.5 to 5.2 do not write.
- **1 and 7 — how the Cycles "engine not available" message is printed.** A plain `Error:` line in 4.5; a timestamped log line in 5.0 to 5.3. Question 7 separates the other test engine's less complete record.
- **7 — forcing with `-E` an engine that is not there.** All five print the same error and write no image; 4.5 then does not exit normally — it dies on the Mac and hangs on Linux — and 5.0 to 5.3 exit 1.
- **10 — which add-ons ship inside Blender.** Fourteen in 4.5, with `copy_global_transform`; the same thirteen in 5.0 to 5.3, without it.
- **11 — camera, microphone and system-audio access on macOS.** Added at 5.1 by a release note, untested; 5.2 and 5.3 are not placed. The permission dialog and limits are under question 11.
- **13 — reading Blender's own messages back from a script.** `bpy.context.window_manager.reports` exists in 5.2 and 5.3 and in no earlier version.
- **13 — the logging arguments.** `--log-list-categories` is refused by 4.5 and accepted by 5.0 to 5.3, with two smaller help-text splits beside it.

### What is not established

- **Every tested answer is limited to its machines and small test jobs.** Windows was untested throughout. Question 12 states the render limits; the machines are named above.
- **1 — reading a `.blend` outside Blender** for add-on identifiers.
- **2 — other ways a menu add-on might change a render**, what the registerable-class lists mean, and what a 5.x render using the old EEVEE name actually does. 5.3 was not offered that name.
- **2 — `-E help` with no display server.** Untried in every version; hiding an existing display is a different condition.
- **3 — no graphics hardware, or a physically unplugged screen.** Neither was tested here in any version. No display server was tested in 4.5 to 5.2 only.
- **3 and 4 — other makes of graphics hardware, and an add-on's device settings with several cards.** These settings were not tested beyond the Mac's Apple graphics and one Linux NVIDIA card.
- **4 — `macos-x64` beyond the tested refusals**, and carrying a set-up add-on to another kind of computer in 5.3.
- **5 and 8 — a third-party extensions site**, and a successful internet fetch in 5.3. A folder repository was tested instead.
- **5 and 8 — the active-repository menu in 4.5, 5.0, 5.1 and 5.3.** Its contents were obscured; 5.2's picture cannot fill them in.
- **5 — what the blocklist does.** Its presence was observed; its use was not tested, and a documentation change is not established.
- **8 — sales and the licence terms for copying**, and what an extension's row shows after a remote install. A file-installed extension's row was opened in 5.3 only.
- **10 — a complete inventory**, including switched-off legacy add-ons, and asking one Blender about another's add-ons.
- **11 — permission enforcement**, other needs legacy add-ons can declare, and a Python route to an installed manifest.
- **12 — the windowed frame-handler install.** The earlier Mac builds and the 5.2.2/5.3 controls give different results, with the earlier render-start method unrecorded.
- **12 — installs in a second Blender in 5.3**, a no-window install with the interface lock on in 5.3, and a windowed install with that lock read back in 4.5 to 5.2.
- **12 — general safety, writes into Blender's own installation folder, and why the tested installs left renders unchanged.** The user-repository tests settle none of these. 5.0's documented preference-save timing is also unresolved.
- **13 — the extensions error status**, a way to know before use whether the needed part of an add-on works, and why the legacy metadata reads carry different added keys.
- **14 — sharing a compiled part across the Python 3.11/3.13 boundary**, two different builds of one two-number version sharing a folder, and two operating-system builds on one computer.
- **14 — every crossing to another version in 5.3**, two copies of 5.3 with and without `portable`, and its computed default folder with nothing overridden. The default folder was seen only during the missing-folder case under question 6.
- **5.3 results with no matching earlier-machine result.** `use_hiprt` on the Mac (11), and hand-made wheels on the Mac set beside compiled wheels on Linux (4). Neither establishes a version difference.
- **Window results split by machine or build.** The settings menu (5) and four missing built-in Add-ons rows (10) match in 5.2.2 and 5.3 on the Mac, against earlier Linux results. The cause is open.
- **Results seen in 5.2.2 and 5.3, incompletely recorded earlier.** A Python-incompatible package left on disk (14), a switch-on's ordinary-output line and warnings during the two refresh calls (13). No version change is established.
- **Results tried in 5.3 alone.** Script switch-on persistence (3), online-access gate cases (5), missing settings folders (6), windowed error routing (7), an extension's opened row (8, 11, 13), a main-loop install during a windowed render (12), the default `addon_utils.enable` collection result (13), and a legacy minimum-version warning (14). Their questions hold the results; none establishes a version difference.

### Where only the documentation differs

- **2 and 11 — the Python overview's metadata instruction.** `bl_info` in 4.5 and 5.0; a manifest in 5.2. Both metadata forms were read in every version; question 11 gives the wording and coverage limits.
- **11 and 13 — the manual's online-access override spelling.** A typo in 4.5 and 5.0, corrected in 5.1; the tested property is the same in all five. Question 11 gives the spellings and coverage limits.

## 1. Can a `.blend` file be read for the add-ons it needs?

**How it is known.** No version's sources answer the question as asked; in 5.3 too, reading a file without Blender is not in the sources, and reading one open in Blender is an inference. The workspace properties and their interface purpose are documented; the step that the workspace's add-on list is stored inside the file is an inference rather than a documented statement. Hand tests settle it by observation: in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux.

**The same in all five.** Nothing in any version's manual, API documentation or release notes describes a record inside a `.blend` file of the add-ons that file needs, and nothing describes a way to read such a record out of one.

The nearest real thing is the same everywhere it was tested. A workspace can carry a list of add-on names — Blender's own "Filter Add-ons" control — and that list does live in the `.blend`. Run with no window, in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Mac and then on Linux, and in 5.3 on the Mac, the test printed the same each time: the three workspace properties exist; switching the filter on works; the operator that adds an add-on to the list wrote `cycles` against the workspace that was *active* (Layout) rather than the one the script had in hand (Animation); the name survived the save; and it read back out of the file with no window. Every version's manual calls this a filter for what the interface shows, not a statement of what the file requires.

One more result came back the same in 4.5 to 5.2 on both machines and in 5.3 on the Mac: the sample count a file was saved with comes back exactly as saved whenever the add-on is switched on, and reads as nothing at all while it is switched off, with the file never touched in between. And the engine name the scene stores is the engine's own identifier, `CYCLES`, not the add-on's module name, `cycles` — read back in 4.5 to 5.2 and again in 5.3, whose sources leave that step as an inference.

**Where it differs, version by version.** What a Blender *without* the add-on reports about that same file. Both machines were run in 4.5 to 5.2 and agreed, version for version; 5.3 was run on the Mac.

**Blender 4.5.** Opening a file saved with Cycles, in a settings folder where Cycles is switched off, reports the engine as `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT`, gives `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` as the only value the engine setting lists, and — this is the part that matters — still lists `cycles` among the scene's keys. The add-on's own settings block survives in the file as a bare key that can be listed.

**Blender 5.0, 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3.** The same file reports the engine as `BLENDER_EEVEE`, gives `BLENDER_EEVEE` as the only listed value, and lists **no keys at all**. The add-on's settings block cannot be seen this way. 5.0's release notes state the change: "Removing these properties from the deprecated 'dict-like' user properties in IDs etc. will fully break forward compatibility", and separately, "`bpy.context.scene['cycles']` will not give access to Cycles' scene settings."

The message printed alongside also differs — it is under question 7.

**A second version difference, at the window.** The add-on list behind the "Filter Add-ons" control shows a row per add-on, each carrying the add-on's category before its name and an empty tick box of its own. **It holds eight rows in 4.5, 5.0 and 5.1 and three rows in 5.2 and 5.3** — in 5.3, glTF 2.0, Cycles and Pose Library. 4.5 to 5.2 were run at the window on the one-card Linux machine, where the Properties editor's Workspace panel showed the tick box itself in all four. The Creator's own run on the Mac covered 5.2 and gave three rows. In 5.3, on the Mac, the Workspace section was drawn closed and a script cannot open it, so the tick box itself was not seen; the list behind it was opened with Blender's own operator and gave three rows. So the two machines agree for 5.2, 5.3 agrees with 5.2 on the Mac, and there is no Mac result for 4.5 to 5.1 and no Linux result for 5.3. The set of add-ons that is switched on is the same eight in all five versions, so the shorter list is not a shorter set of add-ons.

**Verdict: it differs.**

**Not established.** Whether anything outside Blender can read a `.blend` for identifiers and work backwards to an add-on. No version's sources describe the file format at all, and no test on any machine tried it.

## 2. What makes a `.blend` file need an add-on at the moment it renders, and can an add-on that takes part in producing the render be told apart from one that only rearranges Blender's existing tools while the artist works?

**How it is known.** The first half is an inference in every version, built out of documented and observed pieces. The second half is not in the sources in 4.5 to 5.2; 5.3's sources answer it only by inference from what the manifest does and does not hold. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux.

**The same in all five, for the first half.** No source in any version states, in one place, what makes a file need an add-on. All five assemble the same answer from the same pieces, and the joining step is nobody's but the reader's:

- A scene stores which engine it renders with, by name.
- Engines can come from add-ons — every version's manual says third-party renderers are available as add-ons, and Cycles is itself one.
- An add-on hangs its own settings blocks onto Blender's data when it is switched on and deletes them when it is switched off. Cycles does this in every version's files. Ten attachment points were counted — scene, camera, material, light, world, mesh, curve, metaball, object and view layer — and the count is established for 5.1 and 5.2 alone; in 4.5 four were named with others said to exist, in 5.0 only the scene was recorded, and 5.3's record names scenes, materials, lights, worlds and objects "and more".

Three things were then settled by test, identically in 4.5 to 5.2 on both machines, and the same in 5.3 on the Mac:

- The list `-E help` prints can be asked for from the command line. It shrinks when Cycles is switched off, and grows when a test add-on supplying an engine is installed. **It is not the whole of what `-E` will accept**: in 5.0, 5.1 and 5.2, `-E BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` renders at exit 0 even though the printed list holds `BLENDER_EEVEE` and not `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT`; 5.3 was not asked. Anything that decides an engine is unavailable because it is not in the printed list will be wrong.
- The engine setting's own list of values is **not** where add-on engines appear. The route that does change with what is switched on is the set of registered render-engine subclasses, which printed `[('HydraRenderEngine', None), ('CyclesRender', 'CYCLES')]` before switching Cycles off and `[('HydraRenderEngine', None)]` after. `has_multiple_engines` exists and reads true in all five.
- An add-on's whole declared metadata block is readable from Python, for extensions and for older add-ons alike. Cycles' block came back character for character the same on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2.

**Which name works depends on how it is set.** The version's own name — `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` in 4.5, `BLENDER_EEVEE` in 5.0 to 5.3 — is the one name that works by every route in 4.5 to 5.2. On the command line, 4.5 takes `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` only and refuses `BLENDER_EEVEE`, while 5.0, 5.1 and 5.2 take both; set from a script it is the other way about in those three, where `BLENDER_EEVEE` is accepted and `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` is refused. Settled under **Settings 13**. Two limits travel with it: the command-line acceptance rests on one run on one machine on a 64 by 64 scene, and has never been tried on the Mac or on either one-card Linux machine; and whether a 5.x command-line run under `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` really renders in EEVEE, and whether it warns, was never captured. This topic never offered 5.3 the old name by any route.

**The same in all five, for the second half.** Nothing marks an add-on as taking part in producing an image. The only markers are ones the author writes about themselves: the manifest's `type` (only "add-on" or "theme"), its `tags` (a fixed list that holds both `Render` and `User Interface`), its `permissions` (files, network, clipboard, camera, microphone — nothing about rendering), and the older `bl_info` category. Nothing checks any of them against what the add-on does. A test add-on that was not an engine took part in a render in every version — its own line appeared among the render's output — and when it was absent the render said nothing about it at all, finished, and exited 0. Both machines in 4.5 to 5.2; the Mac in 5.3.

**One thing the one-card Linux machine alone can say.** Asked for the engine list with no display for Blender to reach, `-E help` prints no engine list at all in 4.5 to 5.2: it prints a display failure and stops, and 4.5 then hangs where 5.0, 5.1 and 5.2 exit 1. So on that machine this one command needs a display even though it prints nothing but text. **The Mac was never asked this** — no run there hid the display from Blender, in any version — so this is a Linux finding about that command, not a difference between the two operating systems and not a difference between versions. **And the condition it was got under is the narrower one**: that machine has a display server and was merely pointed away from it. Neither machine with no display server at all was ever asked this, and 5.3 was never asked it anywhere.

**Where it differs, version by version.** Both machines were run in 4.5 to 5.2 and agreed on the engine names; 5.3 was run on the Mac.

**Blender 4.5.** The engine list reads `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT`, `BLENDER_WORKBENCH`, `CYCLES`. Asking for that list also stops the program from ending properly: on the Mac it dies with `libc++abi: terminating` and `Abort trap: 6`, exit status 134; on Linux it prints the list and then never exits, and had to be killed — the 124 that run came back with is the time-limit tool's own number and not Blender's, as under question 7.

**Blender 5.0, 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3.** The engine list reads `BLENDER_EEVEE`, `BLENDER_WORKBENCH`, `CYCLES`, and the command exits 0 — on both machines in 5.0 to 5.2, on the Mac in 5.3.

**Verdict: it differs.**

**Not established.** Whether an add-on that only adds menus can still change a render by some other route than the handlers tested here. Not answered in any version, on any machine. Nor was anything tested about what either of the two lists of registerable class types the sources give actually means.

**At the window.** Nothing in an add-on's opened row in the preferences says whether the add-on takes part in a render. The two built-in rows opened do not even carry the same fields as each other — Cycles shows no maintainer and no version, Node Wrangler shows both — but neither carries a tag, a category, a licence, a repository or a declared permission, and both read `Type  Core`. In 5.3 an installed extension declaring the tag `Render` was opened too: its row shows a description, `Website`, `Type  Extension`, `Maintainer`, `Version` and `File`, and no tag. Opening a file whose engine's add-on is switched off shows a warning at the bottom of the screen and offers only EEVEE and Workbench in the engine list. Run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac; the Mac runs agree with each other, the two machines agree for 5.2, and the Linux runs came back the same in all four of their versions. One thing differs with the machine rather than the version: Cycles' own row shows a line of compute-device buttons naming that machine's kinds of graphics hardware — `None`, `CUDA`, `OptiX`, `HIP`, `oneAPI` on the one-card Linux machine, with `None` chosen; `None` and `Metal` on the Mac in 5.2 and 5.3, with `Metal` chosen.

## 3. Can an add-on be installed, switched on, and given whatever else it needs before it will work, on a computer with no screen attached?

**How it is known.** Installing and switching on are documented in every version. Setting an add-on's own settings from a script is an inference in the sources rather than a documented statement. 5.3's sources document installing and switching on by command without a window, infer that this works with no display at all and that the change is kept, and say nothing on whether a script's switch-on is saved by itself. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac, and again on the one-card Linux machine, which also added a case the Mac could not pose. **Two later machines then went further, in 4.5 to 5.2.** The two-card machine, which has no display server at all, ran the whole cycle again — this is the machine that answers the question in the heading, as far as any machine here can. The four-card machine ran one nine-step test, on what `--addons` does. **5.3 was tested on the Mac alone, with no window.**

**The same in all five.** Yes for installing and yes for switching on, with no window, by several documented routes that are word for word the same in every version: the `--command` form implies background mode; `install` and `install-file` do the installing; `-e` switches the add-on on in the same step; `--addons` switches one on at launch; and a startup script can switch add-ons on for every user. There is an operator for it too, `bpy.ops.preferences.addon_enable`, cited in every version's sources but 5.2's; for 5.2 that one route rests on the test rather than on the page.

Tested with no window, the results were identical in 4.5 to 5.2 **and identical on both machines**, and 5.3 gave the same on the Mac:

- Building and installing a small test add-on works, and the switch-on survives into a later, separate run with no explicit save — the install writes the preferences itself. In 4.5 to 5.2 the built file is even the same size on both machines.
- With `--no-prefs` the package is installed but is **not** switched on; it still appears as installed in the package listing. What that flag spares is under question 12.
- The switch-on, switch-off and refresh operators all work and all report finished.
- `--addons` loads the add-on and does **not** put it in the switched-on list; an extension is named there by its long name, `bl_ext.<repository>.<id>`. Given a name that does not exist — an extension's short name included — it prints `Add-on not loaded: "…", cause: No module named '…'` and carries on, exit 0.
- An add-on's own settings can be read, changed and saved from a script, and come back changed in a separate later run. Cycles' list of settings is the same nine names in all five versions, on every machine that read it.
- Fetching from the internet is refused unless online access is allowed, in identical words in all five.

**One result from 5.3 alone: a switch-on made by a script, with no window and no save, is not kept.** In 5.3 on the Mac, a script switched an installed extension on with `bpy.ops.preferences.addon_enable`; the preferences read as changed, and the save-on-exit preference read as on, yet the very next run did not have the add-on switched on. With `bpy.ops.wm.save_userpref()` in the same script, the next run did. So the install command's own `-e` and a script's switch-on behave differently: the first writes the preferences itself, the second only with an explicit save. Never tried in 4.5 to 5.2.

**A first step past the Mac, from the one-card Linux machine, in 4.5 to 5.2.** That machine can be given a Blender with no display to reach at all. Every step above still worked with no display and exited 0: installing, switching on, setting an add-on's own setting and saving it. Then Blender was started **without** the no-window switch, still with no display: it printed the display failure, never ran the script, and stopped — 4.5 hung and had to be killed, and 5.0, 5.1 and 5.2 exited 1. **That machine had a display server running, though, and was merely pointed away from it**, which is a narrower thing than the question asks.

### On a computer with no display server at all — the two-card machine, in 4.5 to 5.2

It has no display server: the display settings are empty, the session is a plain terminal, and no display process is running. The two graphics cards sit there as devices of the kernel, driving nothing.

**Everything worked, identically in all four versions:** building the package, installing it, listing it, switching it on, switching it off, refreshing, reading one of an add-on's own settings, changing it, saving it, and reading it back changed in a separate later run. The build and the install both reported success. Fetching from the internet was refused in the same words as everywhere else, because online access was not allowed. Cycles' list of settings came back as the same nine names as on the other machines.

**So the practical half of this question is answered for 4.5 to 5.2: an add-on can be installed, switched on and set up on a computer with no display server.** It was never run in 5.3. And it does not answer two things in any version: **a computer with no graphics hardware at all** — the cards are there — and a screen being physically unplugged, which nobody could look at on a rented machine and which is not what decides Blender's behaviour anyway. The words used here are the tested ones.

**Ask that machine for a window and it refuses, and the four versions refuse differently.** The same test in 4.5 to 5.2 came back three ways at once: Blender 4.5 wrote its refusal to the error stream and then never ended, and had to be killed; Blender 5.0, 5.1 and 5.2 wrote theirs to the ordinary output stream and exited 1; and Blender 5.0 alone also repeated a warning about the memory checker fourteen times on the error stream. One test, one machine, four versions, so this is the versions differing. It matters to anything that reads Blender's output: both streams have to be watched. 5.3 was never asked.

**One thing in that test is not a fact about the version.** In it, 5.0, 5.1 and 5.2 added a line reporting memory left unfreed and 4.5 did not. Outside that test the line comes and goes in 4.5 to 5.2: 4.5 prints it elsewhere, and the newer three sometimes do not. **Nothing reading Blender's output may treat the line's presence or absence as telling it which version is running**, and the line is not a failed job.

What that machine printed, word for word, is what the one-card machine printed when merely cut off from its display, and the exit statuses match. **That is a coincidence worth naming rather than leaning on**: on what Blender says and does, the two situations look the same, but only one of these machines settles the question.

### Eight switched-on add-ons or nine — the four-card machine in 4.5 to 5.2, and the Mac in 5.3

Observed in 4.5 to 5.2 on the four-card Linux machine and in 5.3 on the Mac. The answer is the same in all five versions:

- **A fresh settings folder has eight add-ons switched on**, and they are the same eight names in every version — printed in the same order in all four versions on the four-card machine. Starting from the factory settings gives the same eight.
- **`--addons` loads the add-on for that one run and does not add it to the switched-on list.** The add-on's own start-up line printed, so it really was loaded, while the list still held only the eight — and the very next run without the flag printed the same eight and no start-up line.
- **Nine names means the saved preferences hold the add-on, and the flag has nothing to do with it.** Once it was switched on and the preferences saved, the list read nine with the flag and nine without it.
- **The settings folder decides the number — except that starting from the factory settings sets the folder aside and always gives eight**, even on a folder whose saved preferences hold the add-on.

**In these tests, the settings folder decides the number.** Eight or nine is not a version difference.

**One thing is about the machine rather than about Blender.** With no window, Cycles' list of offered compute devices comes back **empty** — in 4.5 to 5.2 on both of the first two machines, with a window open as well on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac. The setting's current value is a fact about the machine's own graphics: it reads `METAL` on the Mac, 5.3 included, and `NONE` on Linux, from the same list of settings. Cycles' own `refresh_devices()` fills its devices collection with the machine's own hardware — the NVIDIA card and the processor on the one-card Linux machine in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3 the M1 Pro's processor and its 14-core graphics, with the graphics ticked; in 5.3 the collection read empty before that call. So the empty list was never about Apple's graphics, and none of this is a difference between versions.

**One thing on macOS that a release note settles.** 5.3's release notes say the Blender application icon no longer shows up in the Dock when Blender runs in background mode from the command line. That is a release note saying a thing changed, so it stands as a change at 5.3. No test looked at the Dock in any version.

**Verdict: the same everywhere, for the question as asked.** Installing, switching on and setting an add-on up with no window work in every version: on the Mac in all five, on the one-card Linux machine in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the machine that genuinely has no display server in 4.5 to 5.2. **For 5.3, the no-display-server half rests on its sources' inference, not on a test.** Two neighbouring behaviours do differ between versions — asking that machine for a window, and the Dock icon on macOS — and both are above.

**Not established, in any version.** A computer with **no graphics hardware at all**, and a computer whose screen is physically unplugged. Neither could be reached on any machine **this topic** has had. A machine with no graphics card of any kind is on record all the same — the Windows Server 2025 machine under **Frames 15**, which rendered on it in Blender 4.5 — so the first of the two is untried here rather than out of reach, though that machine was driven over a remote-desktop screen and nothing records what drew it. That is a silence in what could be tried, the same in every version, and not a difference between them.

**One thing worth carrying forward.** 5.1's release notes record that on macOS a system permission dialog is shown when a script or extension reaches for the camera, microphone or system audio. Whether such a dialog can be answered on a machine with no screen is recorded nowhere. See question 11.

## 4. What does an add-on record about the kinds of computer it is meant for, what happens on one it does not name, and does an add-on set up on one computer still work when copied to a computer of a different kind?

**How it is known.** What an add-on records is documented in every version. What happens on a kind it does not name is not in the sources in any of them for installing; the listing step is described, and 5.3's sources call it documented. On copying, the compiled-parts rule is documented in every version, and the consequence for an installed copy is an inference rather than a documented statement. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, which also posed two cases the Mac could not; 5.3 was run on the Mac, which posed one of those two itself. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux. Copying to another kind of computer was not tried in 5.3.

**The same in all five, for what is recorded.** An extension's manifest may carry an optional `platforms` field naming one or more of exactly five names, each pairing an operating system with a processor kind. Leaving the field out means every operating system. Alongside it the manifest records which Blender versions the add-on is for. An older-style add-on records no platform at all — Cycles, which ships with every version, names none.

**The same in all five, for what the documentation says happens elsewhere.** The sources describe filtering *before* the fact, not a refusal after it: a repository is told which machine is asking so that only compatible packages are listed, and Blender's package builder will not produce a package for a kind the manifest does not name. What happens if such a package reaches the machine anyway is not in the sources in any version.

**The same in all five, once tested — and the same on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2.** The hand tests answered the part the documentation left open:

- A package naming only another kind of computer is refused, cleanly, with a message naming both platforms. It reads `ERROR <id>: This platform (macos-arm64) isn't one of (windows-x64)` on the Mac, 5.3 included, and `ERROR <id>: This platform (linux-x64) isn't one of (windows-x64)` on Linux — the same sentence, naming whichever machine is running. Nothing is installed, the repository listing is empty of it afterwards, and **the run still exits 0**. The Linux machine added the case the Mac could not pose: a package built for macOS on Apple Silicon is refused on Linux exactly as a Windows one is, and a package built for Linux installs there without complaint. In 5.3 on the Mac, packages naming Windows, Intel Macs and Linux were all refused this way, and one naming Apple Silicon installed.
- The same package's folder placed straight into the extensions directory *is* listed, but cannot be switched on: the same "is incompatible" error, raised twice and printed once more, exit 0. So the check happens when Blender scans the folder as well as when it installs. The same in every version on every machine that ran it.
- A repository built from a plain folder is filtered by Blender itself: only the package for the machine asking is listed, and asking to install the other by name fails with `Package "smoozy_win_only" not found in remote repositories!`, exit 1. Each machine filters by its own kind.
- Only the bundled module matching the running machine is unpacked. All the bundled files stay unopened in the add-on's own folder; the unpacked code goes elsewhere and is for that machine alone. The Mac unpacked only the macOS file, in every version; Linux unpacked only the Linux one. No file for either other kind appeared anywhere. 5.3's run used hand-made bundled files holding no compiled code, so it shows which file Blender picks by its name and nothing more.
- Building one package per kind of computer refuses a manifest that names no platforms. In 5.3 a manifest naming three kinds built one package for each.

**The result that matters most, and it is not a refusal.** An add-on may name no platforms at all and still carry a compiled part built for another kind of computer — and Blender says nothing. Run on the one-card Linux machine in 4.5 to 5.2, with two such packages: both **installed with no warning of any kind, exit status 0**, both were switched on, and **nothing was unpacked** — the compiled part sat unopened in the add-on's own folder and no unpacking folder was created. The failure comes only when something uses the add-on, and even that run exits 0: it prints `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'PIL'` and ends with a status that says nothing is wrong. A repeat ran eight installs across the four versions and recorded forty-eight exit statuses, every one of them 0. **This is a different case from the clean refusal above and must not be blurred with it**: a package built outright for the wrong machine is refused with a message naming both platforms, while a package carrying a compiled part for the wrong machine is accepted in silence. In 5.3, on the Mac, the same shape came back: a package whose only bundled file was named for Linux, or only for Windows, installed with nothing said, exit 0, was switched on, unpacked nothing, and failed to import when used, exit 0. **Those bundled files held no compiled code and ran on another machine, so 5.3 is not set against 4.5 to 5.2 here** — the two agree, and neither settles the other.

**Carrying an installed, set-up add-on to a machine of a different kind.** This was one test run across both machines, in 4.5 to 5.2: a settings folder holding an installed add-on with its Linux compiled part already unpacked was packed up on the one-card Linux machine and opened by the Mac's Blender of the same version. The Mac listed the add-on as installed and had it switched on; the plain Python part loaded and reported its version; and using it failed with `ImportError: cannot import name '_imaging' from 'PIL'` — **exit status 0 again**. On disk the compiled files were still the Linux ones, and nothing went back to unpack the macOS file sitting beside them. So the install, the switch-on and the plain Python travel; the compiled part does not; and nothing announces it until the add-on is used. **This fails in the same quiet way as the case above**, in all four of those versions. Not tried in 5.3.

**Where it differs, version by version.** Both machines were run in 4.5 to 5.2 and agreed; 5.3 was run on the Mac.

**Blender 4.5 and 5.0.** The refusal's second line reads `Package should have been installed but not found: <id>`.

**Blender 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3.** The refusal's second line reads `Package was expected to be installed but not found: <id>`.

That same split appears again, word for word, when an extension is refused for the Blender version it declares rather than for the kind of computer — under question 14. The first line, naming the platform mismatch, is word for word the same in all five and names whichever machine is running.

The Python each version carries decides which bundled module file suits it and where installing unpacks it: `cp311` and `extensions/.local/lib/python3.11/site-packages/` for 4.5 and 5.0, `cp313` and `.../python3.13/site-packages/` for 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3. The exact builds are under question 14.

**Verdict: it differs.**

**Not established, in any version.** What a Windows machine does with a package meant for another kind. No machine here is a Windows one, and the sources' Windows-only failure message was not reached. A Windows machine can be rented, so this is untried rather than out of reach. **Not established for 5.3:** carrying a set-up add-on to another kind of computer.

**A note, marked clearly as not established.** `macos-x64` is one of the five names a manifest may use, while a requirements page states that Blender 5.0 and later need Apple Silicon; 5.3's manual says the same on its macOS installing page. The hand tests settled the part that could be settled — `macos-x64` is refused on the Apple Silicon Mac in all five versions, and on the one-card Linux machine in 4.5 to 5.2 — and the rest is not established for any version.

**At the window.** Installing the same Windows-only package through the window refuses it, and nothing is listed afterwards. Two routes were used, and they print differently. **Chosen by hand through "Install from Disk…"** — the Creator, on the Mac, in 5.2 — the platform sentence came up as a red warning in the console and the panel did nothing. **Called as the window's own install operator from a script** — on the one-card Linux machine in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3 — the operator reported **finished while refusing the package**, and only the refusal's second line was printed; no line naming the platform reached the terminal. The picture taken afterwards, with the Add-ons list showing, holds no sign of the refused package, on Linux in all four of its versions and on the Mac in 5.3. One thing there is about the machine and not about Blender or the version: Cycles' own panel offers a row of compute-device buttons naming that machine's kinds of graphics hardware, and those buttons are drawn by the add-on's own interface code rather than read from the setting's list of values — the list a script reads is empty on both machines, with a window open and without.

## 5. How can an add-on be found, fetched and installed without a person at a screen, and what decides the places it can be fetched from?

**How it is known.** Documented in every version. 4.5 to 5.2's sources document it without qualification; 5.3's mark one step as inferred — that there is no search command, so finding means listing what the repositories hold — and do not say whether the extension commands obey the online-access setting. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux.

**The same in all five.** The extension command line covers the whole cycle — finding, fetching, installing, updating and removing — and using it forces the no-window mode. Where an add-on may be fetched from is decided by the repositories held in the preferences, by whether Blender is allowed on the internet at all, by any access token the repository demands, and by whether the network in between permits the connection. Repositories can be added, listed and removed from the command line, and a repository's address may be a plain file path rather than a web address.

Tested, the results were identical in 4.5 to 5.2 **and identical on both machines**, and 5.3 on the Mac agreed except where it says otherwise:

- A fresh settings folder holds exactly three repositories: `extensions.blender.org` at `https://extensions.blender.org/api/v1/extensions/` with no access token, `User Default` in the user's own folder, and `System` inside the Blender installation. The only difference between the machines is where that last folder sits on disk. In 5.3, listing and reading them wrote nothing into the settings folder.
- **The subcommands: twelve in 4.5 to 5.2, thirteen in 5.3.** In 4.5 to 5.2, on both machines, word for word: create a listing from packages, build, validate, list, sync, update, install, install from file, remove, list repositories, add repository, remove repository. 5.3 has those twelve and `repo-info`, which shows high-level information about a repository from the listing the last sync fetched. Blender 5.2.2, run on the same Mac the same day, printed the twelve and no `repo-info`, so this is the version and not the machine.
- Installing by web address is not offered in any version — the install command takes package identifiers and nothing else.
- The package listing prints one line per repository and, indented beneath it, one line per package carrying its identifier, name and tagline, marked as installed where it is on the machine. No version number, no switched-on state, and no built-in add-on ever appears in it.
- Syncing repository listings is gated by the online-access preference, not only by the network. The refusal is identical in all five, and it applies even to a repository that is a plain folder on the same machine, until online access is allowed. In 5.3, with `--offline-mode` given, the refusal ends instead by saying to launch Blender without that switch. Installing from an already-synced folder is a separate case, below.
- Asking for updates for a package installed from a file prints nothing of Blender's own and exits 1; with online access forced on it prints only the "sync required" line and exits 0. Nothing is ever offered for the package installed from a file.
- **The settings each repository carries: fourteen in 4.5 to 5.2, sixteen in 5.3.** 5.3 adds `show_expanded` and `use_access_token_for_asset_libraries`. Read on Linux in 4.5 to 5.2.0; Blender 5.2.2 on the same Mac the same day as 5.3 also gave fourteen, so this is the version and not the machine.
- **The listing a repository generates: one more line in 5.3.** Made from a plain folder with `server-generate`, the listing carries each package's whole manifest, its size and a hash in every version, and in 5.3 a top-level line `"assetlib_auth_method": "PER_LIBRARY"` between the blocklist and the packages. 4.5 to 5.2.0 on Linux write no such line, and 5.2.2 on the same Mac the same day wrote none either, so this is the version. What the line governs is not recorded in this topic.

**Two gate results recorded in 5.3 alone, on the Mac.** With the online-access preference saved as on, a sync with no switch went past the gate — it reached for the official address, which a network block then stopped. And once a folder repository had been synced, **installing from it by name worked with the preference off and no switch**: the gate stopped the sync, not the install from a listing already fetched. Neither was tried in 4.5 to 5.2.

**No successful internet fetch was tested in 5.3.** Its fetch attempts were blocked at the network. Its successful repository syncs and installs used a folder on the Mac.

**One more route.** An add-on can also be switched on for a single run at launch, without touching the preferences at all. The four-card machine in 4.5 to 5.2, and the Mac in 5.3, showed that this loads the add-on — its own start-up line prints — leaves the switched-on list untouched, and is gone by the very next run. So a fetch-and-use cycle with no person at a screen can install once and enable per run, or install once and save the preferences, and the two are visibly different states. The full account is under question 3.

**Verdict: it differs.** Three changes are established: 5.3's thirteenth subcommand, its two further repository settings, and the extra line in a generated listing. Each was set against Blender 5.2.2 on the same Mac the same day. The cases tried only in 5.3 establish no change.

**Not established, in any version.** A third-party extensions site. No version's sources name an address for one, so none could be tried without guessing at a URL; a repository made from a folder was tested instead, in all five versions.

**Not established: the blocklist's effect.** A repository's own configuration can carry a blocklist naming packages that are not to be served. The generated listing carries an empty blocklist in every version — on the one-card Linux machine in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3. No test exercised it. Whether 4.5's manual lacks the passage is also not established, so no documentation change follows.

**At the window.** On the Mac, in 5.2, the Get Extensions panel showed the same three repositories with the same addresses, plus three settings the repository listing command does not print — "Check for Updates on Startup", "Clean Files After Install" and a custom-directory tick. In a fresh settings folder the panel shows an online-access prompt rather than a repository list, with two buttons, "Continue Offline" and "Allow Online Access" — on the one-card Linux machine in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3. In 5.3, once the prompt counts as answered, it is replaced by a box headed `Repository Alert:` saying the official repository's remote data is unavailable until online access is allowed or the repository is switched off. On Linux the repositories and their settings were read from the preferences behind the panel, and came back as the same three, each switched on, only the official one carrying an address, none demanding an access token, and only the official one set to check on startup; 5.3 read the same from the preferences on the Mac.

- **The active-repository menu.** Read in 5.2 alone, on the one-card Linux machine: the three repositories with their tick boxes, the address, a ticked "Check for Updates on Startup" and a closed "Advanced" section. In 4.5, 5.0 and 5.1 the menu opened but a tooltip covered its contents in every picture taken; in 5.3, on the Mac, a tooltip covered all of it but its heading and one greyed `Refresh Remote` row. So **what 4.5, 5.0, 5.1 and 5.3 list there is unread, not known** — and it must not be filled in from 5.2.
- **The repositories pop-over**, a separate panel, was opened in 5.3 alone, on the Mac: the three repositories, each ticked, the official address, a ticked "Check for Updates on Startup" and a closed "Advanced" section.
- **The settings menu beside the panel** holds three items on Linux in 4.5 to 5.2.0 — Visit Extensions Platform, a greyed-out Install Available Updates, and Install from Disk — and five on the Mac in 5.3: Visit Extensions Platform, a greyed Refresh Remote, Refresh Local, a greyed Install Available Updates, and Install from Disk. Blender 5.2.2 on the same Mac the same day showed the same five in the same order. **So this is not 5.3 differing**; whether the machine or the build gives the other two items is not established.

## 6. What does an add-on belong to, once it is installed on a computer?

**How it is known.** Documented in every version. The description of the add-on's own writable directory as belonging to it, and the step that the environment variables make ownership work the same way outside the interface, are inferences rather than documented statements; 5.3's sources document each part and infer the summary. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac.

**The same in all five.** An installed add-on belongs to three things at once:

- **A repository**, which is either managed by the user or provided by the system and read-only. Installing from a file always puts it in a local repository, and no updates are then available for it.
- **A directory**, which sits inside a folder named for the Blender version. Older-style add-ons belong to a scripts directory instead.
- **A Python name that carries its repository** — `bl_ext.<repository>.<id>` — which every version's manual explains was done to stop two add-ons of the same name from different repositories clashing.

It may also own a small writable directory of its own, kept across upgrades and removed when the add-on is uninstalled. Every version's manual warns that an add-on must not write into its own folder, because that folder may not be writable and is cleared on upgrade.

Tested, the results were identical in 4.5 to 5.2 **and identical on both machines**, and the same in 5.3 on the Mac where it was asked:

- The installed files land in the repository's folder inside the settings folder, and the manifest on disk is the same text that was packed, unchanged.
- The writable folder Blender hands the add-on is **not** inside the add-on's own folder: it sits under `extensions/.user/<repository>/<id>`.
- Belonging to one Blender version is a matter of the folder and of nothing else. One settings folder was handed to 4.5 to 5.2 in turn, on each machine in its own right; an add-on installed once by 5.2 was listed and switched on by 5.1, 5.0 and 4.5 from that same folder, and the shared folder grew no version-named subfolder. **Nothing in Blender 4.5 to 5.2 refuses an add-on because another version installed it** — it is only the default folder's version-numbered name that keeps versions apart. What Blender does refuse on a version ground is two things: an extension whose manifest declares a Blender range the running Blender is outside, where the install command still exits zero; and a bundled Python package built for another Python, where it exits 1. Both are under question 14, in all five. **5.3 was not handed another version's folder**; its sources describe the same per-version folders.
- The environment variable that moves the settings folder moves all of it, in every version.

**One result from 5.3 alone: a settings folder that does not exist.** In one 5.3 case, `BLENDER_USER_RESOURCES` named a folder that had not been made. Asked for its folders, Blender printed paths inside the missing one; but the preferences it then wrote, holding an extension's switch-on, went into the default 5.3 user folder, and a later run pointed at another missing folder started with that extension switched on. The extension's own files went where `BLENDER_USER_EXTENSIONS` pointed, which did exist. This was not repeated or tried in 4.5 to 5.2.

**Verdict: the same everywhere.** Every version answered, and the answers agree — 4.5 to 5.2 on both machines, 5.3 on the Mac.

The only things that vary are the version number written into the paths and the shape of the paths themselves on each operating system, which is the rule working rather than a difference. One file inside the installed folder is named for the Python version and so differs by version — that is the Python difference recorded under question 14, not a difference in what an add-on belongs to.

## 7. What does a render do when an add-on it needs is not there?

**How it is known.** Not in the sources in any of the five versions — no documented error, no documented fallback, no documented exit status, in any version's manual, API documentation or release notes. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac, and answered it three ways. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux.

**The same in all five, and the same on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2.**

- **A file whose engine is Cycles, rendered with Cycles switched off**, prints the "engine not available" message and then **carries on and renders anyway** with EEVEE at sixty-four samples. An image is written — on the Mac, 3.5 kB against the 4.8 kB of the Cycles one in 4.5 to 5.2, and 3.9 kB against 5.2 kB in 5.3; on the one-card Linux machine, two files a few hundred bytes apart, both around 3 to 5 kB — and **the process exits 0**. Nothing in the exit status says the wrong engine was used.
- **A file whose engine belongs to an add-on that was never installed** behaves the same way: the same message naming that engine, the same EEVEE render, an image written, exit 0. Opening the file in the same bare Blender and asking for its engine gives the same message and then the real-time engine's name.
- **An add-on that changes a render without being an engine** is completely silent when absent. The render prints no message at all about it, finishes normally, and exits 0.

**Where it differs, version by version.** Two things: how the message is printed, and what happens when the engine is forced on the command line and the add-on is not there.

**The Cycles message.** The words are identical in all five. In 4.5 the line reads `Error: Engine 'CYCLES' not available for scene 'Scene' (an add-on may need to be installed or enabled)`. From 5.0 it is a timestamped log line instead: `00:00.407  reports          | ERROR Engine 'CYCLES' not available for scene 'Scene' (an add-on may need to be installed or enabled)`. Anything that reads Blender's own output has to match both shapes. The same on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2; the timestamped form in 5.3 on the Mac. **The other test engine's formatting is less settled:** the 5.0 to 5.2 Mac excerpts give `ERROR Engine 'SMOOZY_TEST'…`, while their Linux excerpts give `Error: Engine 'SMOOZY_TEST'…`. The 5.3 Mac run gives a timestamped line. Those excerpts do not establish one uniform format for every missing engine, or a version change between those readings.

**The forced engine.** All five print `Error: engine not found 'CYCLES'` and write no image — on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, on the Mac in 5.3.

**Blender 4.5.** The process does not end properly. On the Mac it is killed: `libc++abi: terminating`, `Abort trap: 6`, exit status 134. On the one-card Linux machine it hangs instead — it never exits on its own and was killed after five minutes. **The 124 in the record is the time-limit tool's own number, not Blender's** — the run was wrapped in a five-minute limit and 4.5 never ended. Nothing matching endings by number should add 124 to the list of things Blender hands back. The difference from the other versions is real on both machines; the symptom is not the same on both.

**Blender 5.0, 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3.** The process exits 1 — on both machines in 5.0 to 5.2, and in 5.3 on the Mac, three times out of three.

The engine that is fallen back to also differs by name — `BLENDER_EEVEE_NEXT` in 4.5, `BLENDER_EEVEE` in 5.0 to 5.3. Confirmed on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3.

**Verdict: it differs.**

**Not established, in any version.** Whether any of this behaves the same on Windows.

**At the window.** The same sentence appears as a red warning along the bottom of the screen, and it appears as soon as the file is opened rather than when a render is started. The Render Engine field reads `EEVEE`, and the engine the file was saved with is not among the choices offered — the list holds exactly two entries, EEVEE and Workbench. Run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac; every run agrees. Note that the field reads `EEVEE` in every version even though the name behind it differs, so the version difference recorded above is not visible at the window. **In 5.3, with the window open, the message did not reach the terminal at all**; it sat in `bpy.context.window_manager.reports`. Where 4.5 to 5.2 sent it with a window open was not recorded.

## 8. Where do add-ons come from besides Blender's own, how are they sold, and what does that mean for taking a copy of one from one computer to another?

**How it is known.** Where add-ons come from is documented in every version; how they are sold is not in the sources in any of them; the technical limits on copying are documented in every version. The licence conclusion about copying is an inferred step, and it is treated below as not settled. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux.

**The same in all five, for where they come from.** The official platform is extensions.blender.org; other sites are supported as long as they follow the same specification, in three grades of effort — a static listing file, a listing served on demand, or a whole forked platform. A package can also simply be a file installed from disk, or come from anywhere on the web, and Blender ships with some already. A shared read-only system repository, which may sit on a network drive, is the documented way to give a whole studio the same set.

Tested, the results were identical in 4.5 to 5.2 **and identical on both machines**, and the same in 5.3 on the Mac: three repositories set up to begin with; a listing generated from a plain folder carries each package's whole manifest, its size and a hash (5.3 adds one line, under question 5); that folder can be added as a repository by file address and its packages installed by name; and a package installed from a file never gets an update offered.

**The same in all five, for how they are sold.** Not in the sources. No version's manual, API documentation or release notes describes selling, pricing, purchase, payment, licence keys, activation or entitlement. No test on a computer can answer it. The only access-control mechanism described anywhere is a personal access token a repository may demand, and no version's sources connect it to payment.

**What copying actually does, in 4.5 to 5.2.** A settings folder holding an installed, set-up add-on was packed on the one-card Linux machine and opened by the Mac's Blender of the same version. The add-on was listed as installed and was switched on; its plain Python part worked; its compiled part did not, and the failure came only when the add-on was used, at exit status 0. The full account is under question 4. So the technical limit the manual states is not only documented — it has been seen, across the project's two machines, in all four of those versions. Not tried in 5.3.

**The licence side is not settled, in any version.** What is documented is a licence rule of the official platform only — add-ons there must be under the GNU General Public License v3.0 or later, and assets used in them must be public domain — plus a required licence declaration in every manifest, whatever the source. 4.5 to 5.2 record all of that; 5.3's record carries the add-on licence rule and the required licence field. **Whether an add-on from the official platform may be copied between your own machines is not settled**: the platform's licence rule and the manual's statement that that licence carries the right to copy and distribute are both documented, and nothing in any version's sources joins them; 5.3's, like some earlier ones, takes the step as an inference. Joining them is a step, and it would not extend to add-ons from anywhere else in any case.

**Verdict: not established.** Where add-ons come from is answered and agrees everywhere; the two repository changes affecting this answer are under question 5 and in the behaviour list. How add-ons are sold is not in the sources in any version. Copying is answered for its technical side in 4.5 to 5.2 and agrees, is untried in 5.3, and its licence side is settled nowhere.

**Observed in the Cycles 5.1 source files.** Its licence folder names Apache 2.0 as the default licence for Cycles' source. This is one bundled add-on's source licence in 5.1, not a licence finding for every version or for other add-ons.

**At the window.** A built-in add-on's own row shows no licence and no repository name, so a person cannot tell from it what they are allowed to do with a copy; and the only setting on the repositories side bearing on getting at an add-on is an access-token tick box, which is off for all three repositories in a fresh settings folder. Run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac; every run agrees. **In 5.3 an installed extension's row was opened too** — one installed from a file into User Default, whose manifest declares a licence and a copyright line: it shows `Website`, `Type  Extension`, `Maintainer`, `Version` and a `File` path running through `extensions/user_default/`, and **no licence, no copyright and no repository name**. What an installed extension's row shows in 4.5 to 5.2 is unsettled, and what a row shows for one fetched from a remote repository is unsettled in every version.

## 10. Can a computer be asked which add-ons it holds, and for which Blender?

**How it is known.** The listing routes are documented in every version and a complete inventory is not in the sources in any of them. The step that the answer is naturally per Blender version is an inference in some versions' sources, 5.3's included, and read straight off the directory layout in others. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.3 on the Mac. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux.

**The same in all five.** Yes, and with no window — but by three different routes, none of which is everything, and all three behave identically in every version: on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, on the Mac in 5.3.

- **What the repositories hold.** The package listing command shows an installed package **whether or not the add-on is switched on** — after switching one off and saving the preferences, the same "installed" line was still printed. It never shows a built-in add-on, because those are not packages in a repository.
- **What is switched on.** The preferences' add-on collection **drops an add-on the moment it is switched off**. So it is the switched-on set, not an inventory. In a fresh settings folder it holds the same eight in all five versions: `bl_pkg`, `cycles`, `io_anim_bvh`, `io_curve_svg`, `io_mesh_uv_layout`, `io_scene_fbx`, `io_scene_gltf2`, `pose_library`. The count is eight and it does not vary by version; what varies is the settings folder, and all of that is under question 3.
- **What is on the machine at all.** The `addon_utils` module lists every add-on that ships with Blender, switched on or not, with each one's whole metadata block, and an installed extension appears in the same list by its full name. It is the closest thing to an inventory found in any version. It is used by every version's manual and documented in no version's Python API — searched for and absent in 4.5, 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3; for 5.0 the absence is not established.

Two further results were identical in every version. The system-information report can be written with no window and with no prompt of any kind, and its "Enabled add-ons" section names each add-on's version and path, an installed extension included, with the version reading `UNKNOWN` where the metadata block carries none — Cycles among them. It behaves the same in all five on their tested machines. And which Blender the answer belongs to is a matter of the folder alone, exactly as under question 6; 5.3 was not asked about another version.

**Where it differs, version by version.** Not the routes, which are the same — what comes back through them. Both machines were run in 4.5 to 5.2 and agreed; 5.3 was run on the Mac.

**Blender 4.5.** The bundled add-on folder holds **fourteen** add-ons, including `copy_global_transform`.

**Blender 5.0, 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3.** It holds **thirteen**, and `copy_global_transform` is not among them. The thirteen are the same names, in the same order, in all four.

**Verdict: it differs.**

**Not established, in any version.** Whether any route gives one complete inventory of everything installed, including switched-off older-style add-ons. Whether one Blender can be asked about another Blender's add-ons — the path lookup takes a version number and returns a path, but no version's sources say the add-ons found there can then be listed.

**At the window.** The Add-ons list holds add-ons that ship with Blender, each with a tick box, and the ticked ones are the ones the no-window run reports as switched on; the extensions system itself is in that switched-on list but has no row of its own. On the one-card Linux machine, in 4.5 to 5.2.0, the list holds a row for every add-on that ships. **On the Mac, in 5.3 and in 5.2.2 run beside it, it draws no row for four of them** — the BVH, FBX, SVG and UV Layout add-ons — although all four are among the eight switched on. Since 5.2.2 on the same Mac draws the same as 5.3, this is not 5.3 differing; whether it is the machine or the build is not established. An opened built-in row shows a version and a maintainer for one add-on and neither for Cycles — which matches the `UNKNOWN` the report gives from outside the window — and shows no repository name and no location on disk. The rows of older-style add-ons placed by hand do show a `File` path, on the Mac in every version, and in 5.3 an installed extension's row does too; none shows a repository name. Run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac.

## 11. Can an add-on depend on something else being on the computer, or on a computer elsewhere, before it will work, and can what it needs be read anywhere?

**How it is known.** The self-containment rule and the network permission are documented in every version. Whether an add-on can declare that it needs another add-on is answered from the same field list in every version, with different degrees of confidence — the absence of such a field is read as an inference from an exhaustive list in some versions' sources, 5.3's included, and as documented in others. The absence of a Python route to read an installed add-on's manifest is not in the sources in any of them. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the Creator's Mac and again on the one-card Linux machine, which also posed a case the Mac could not, and in 5.3 on the Mac. The window half was run in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux.

**The same in all five.** The documented position is the opposite of a dependency system: an add-on packaged as an extension **must not** depend on anything else already being on the computer — it has to bring what it needs with it. 4.5 to 5.2's manuals give the same three ways to comply: bundle Python modules (recommended), bundle the other add-on inside your own, or fold a pure-Python dependency in as a sub-module; 5.3's record quotes the rule naming third-party Python modules, and bundling other add-ons together. Every version's manual names "an add-on depends on another add-on" as a real case and answers it with bundling rather than with a declaration. No manifest field in any version names another extension.

On depending on a computer elsewhere, all five agree: an add-on may declare that it needs the network, and must check the property that says whether Blender is allowed online. Every version's manual carries the same caveat — Blender cannot prevent a third-party add-on from breaking that rule. The preference is off by default and can be forced either way from the command line. Separately, the machine an add-on comes *from* may be elsewhere and may demand an access token, but that matters only for installing and updating.

On reading what an add-on needs, all five agree: it can be read from the manifest, a plain file that ships inside the package and sits beside the installed add-on at a known path. **A route through Blender's Python interface is not established in any version.** The sources name none; that does not establish that none exists.

Tested, the results were identical in 4.5 to 5.2 **and identical on both machines**, and the same in 5.3 on the Mac:

- Only one of the two spellings of the online-access override property exists: `bpy.app.online_access_override`. `bpy.app.online_access_overriden` does not exist. Tested on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3.
- Forcing Blender online or offline changes what the add-on reads and **never touches the preference itself**.
- Validating a package prints exactly one line — that the manifest parsed — and no manifest field at all. So that command cannot be used in any version to read an add-on's declared needs.
- A bundled module is unpacked into a shared place, not into the add-on's own folder, and can then be used from a plain run with no window. Each machine unpacked its own kind of file.
- The manifest on disk is the same text that was packed, and a generated repository listing carries the same fields plus the package's size and a hash.

**Two documentation differences, without a tested behaviour difference.** The Python overview in 4.5 and 5.0 says add-ons must carry a `bl_info` variable for metadata such as name, author, category and project link. In 5.2 it says add-on extensions must carry a `blender_manifest.toml`, naming tags in place of category. That sentence's wording is not established for 5.1 or 5.3. Both metadata forms were read in every version: a full `bl_info` block for each bundled add-on and a manifest from an installed extension, on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2 and on the Mac in 5.3. The wording does not establish a change in which forms Blender accepts.

The online-access manual in 4.5 and 5.0 spells the property `bpy.app.online_access_overriden`; 5.1 spells it `bpy.app.online_access_override`, as every version's API page does, including 5.3's. The tests above establish a manual typo. That manual sentence's spelling is not established for 5.2 or 5.3.

**The one thing the one-card Linux machine settled that the Mac could not, in 4.5 to 5.2.** What happens when the bundled module the running machine needs is not in the package. The answer is that nothing happens at install time: the add-on installs with no warning, exit status 0, is switched on, and **nothing is unpacked at all**. The failure arrives only when something uses it — `ModuleNotFoundError` — and that run exits 0 as well. So an add-on may declare a bundled module, be installed, be switched on, and simply not have it. The same shape of failure appears when a set-up add-on is carried between the two machines; both are recorded under question 4. 5.3 posed this case on the Mac itself, with hand-made bundled files holding no compiled code, and got the same shape — question 4 says why the two are not set against each other.

**Where it differs, version by version.** Both machines were run in 4.5 to 5.2 and agreed on the Python versions; 5.3 was read on the Mac.

**Blender 4.5 and 5.0.** A bundled module must be built for Python 3.11, and installing unpacks it into the Python 3.11 folder. Neither version records any way for a script or extension to reach the machine's camera, microphone or system audio.

**Blender 5.1.** A bundled module must be built for Python 3.13, and installing unpacks it into the Python 3.13 folder; 5.1's release notes state the Python upgrade. 5.1's release notes also state, in so many words, that on macOS scripts and extensions can **now** access the device camera, microphone and system audio, and that a system permission dialog is shown to the person. That is a release note saying a thing was added, so it stands as a change at 5.1; no test touched it in any version.

**Blender 5.2 and 5.3.** Python 3.13, as 5.1 — and the same Python build in both. That supplies no Python-version difference between them. Whether 5.2 or 5.3 still has the camera, microphone and system-audio access was never recorded either way, and nothing anywhere records it being taken away.

The exact Python builds are under question 14.

A third difference sits in Cycles' own settings: `use_hiprt` reads false in 4.5 and 5.0 and true in 5.1 and 5.2. It rests on two machines — read in 4.5 to 5.2 on the one-card Linux machine, and again on the two-card machine, with the same split both times. The Mac run in 4.5 to 5.2 recorded Cycles' list of settings and the values of some of them, but not the values of `use_hiprt` and `use_oneapirt`. **In 5.3, on the Mac, both read true.** Since no earlier version's value was recorded on the Mac and 5.3 never ran on Linux, 5.3 is not set against the others: whether 5.3 keeps 5.1's and 5.2's value on Linux, and whether the earlier split appears on macOS, are both untested. The list of settings itself is the same nine in every version on every machine that read it, and one of them names Apple's own graphics on the one-card Linux machine too.

**Verdict: it differs.**

**Not established, in any version.** Whether a declared permission is enforced anywhere — no version's sources say it is, and the one enforcement-shaped sentence in every version says the opposite. What an older-style add-on can record about what it needs, beyond its metadata block, which has no field for it in any version.

**A note on one release note in 4.5 alone.** 4.5's bug-fix notes carry a line about a Python library dependency in extensions failing to load from subprocesses. It is a one-line fix entry, its wording points at Windows, and it was not tried on any machine in any version. It is recorded here rather than read as a behaviour difference.

**At the window.** No declared permission, no bundled module and no dependency of any kind appears in an add-on's opened row; the only field naming anything outside Blender is a website address. Run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the one-card Linux machine, and in 5.2 and 5.3 on the Mac; every run agrees. In 4.5 to 5.2 neither add-on opened declares a permission in the first place, so those runs settle what those rows show and nothing more. **In 5.3 an installed extension declaring two permissions, network and files, was opened, and neither appears in its row.** Alongside it, the System preferences carry a Network section with an "Allow Online Access" tick box, off in a fresh settings folder, which is the preference the online-access property reads; in 5.3 the same section also shows a time-out of 10 and a connection limit of 5.

## 12. Does installing an add-on disturb a render already running on the same computer?

**How it is known.** Not in the sources, in any of the five versions. No manual page, API page, release note or Cycles file in any version puts installing and rendering in the same sentence. There is no statement that it is safe, none that it is unsafe, and none describing any effect at all. So every word of the answer below comes from hand tests. In 4.5 to 5.2 they were run on the RTX 4080 SUPER machine — the fourth rented Linux machine — and again on the Creator's Mac; one, which needs a screen, was run on the Mac alone; and one, on the Linux machine alone, put the interface-lock test in a form that could come out either way. **In 5.3 they were run on the Mac alone, one Blender at a time**, with no window and with a window.

**Every version reads the question two ways.** Reading A is a render and an install inside one and the same Blender. Reading B is a render in one Blender and an install fired at it from another. No version's sources answer either reading. The tests answered both in 4.5 to 5.2, and reading A alone in 5.3.

**Scale of the evidence.** Across this topic, no render was larger than 640 by 480, none longer than eight frames, and all were PNG. The engines were Cycles, EEVEE, Workbench and a one-colour test engine; one small test add-on was used at a time. These runs do not establish what happens with larger jobs or other add-ons.

**Reading A with no window — the same in all five.** A handler installed a package after the second frame, from inside the rendering Blender. It returned `{'FINISHED'}`, the add-on appeared in that Blender's switched-on collection at once, the render carried on through frame eight, and every frame matched the undisturbed control picture for picture — the two images loaded into Blender and their pixel values compared one by one. On both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3.

**Reading A with a window, the install fired from the render's own frame handler — not settled.** In 4.5.12, 5.0.1, 5.1.2 and 5.2.0 on the Mac, with Blender open on the screen and driven by its own commands rather than by clicking, this install returned `{'FINISHED'}`, all eight frames were written, and every frame matched the no-window control. In 5.2.2 and 5.3 on the same Mac, with the render started the way the window's own Render Animation starts it, the same install **raised** — `ValueError: signal only works in main thread of the main interpreter` — and installed nothing, while the render went on and every frame matched the control; in 5.3 that was so with the interface lock off and with it on. How the earlier runs started their render is not recorded, so the two readings cannot be set against each other. **Since 5.2.2 does what 5.3 does, this is not 5.3 differing from 5.2.**

**Reading A with a window and the lock read back — 5.3 alone.** In 5.3 the install was then fired from Blender's main loop, by a timer five seconds into an eight-frame render at 512 samples, so the render was still going. With the window open it returned `{'FINISHED'}` after frame 2 with the render running — with the lock off, and with it on, read back as on at the moment the install fired — and every frame matched a no-window control pixel for pixel. So in 5.3 on the Mac, neither a window, nor the lock, nor the two together change reading A when the install comes from the main loop. Never run in 4.5 to 5.2.

**The same in 4.5 to 5.2, on both machines unless said; untried in 5.3.**

- **Reading B — installs fired at a running render change nothing.** An eight-frame Cycles animation was rendered twice, once undisturbed and once with install-and-remove pairs of a small test extension fired at it from a second Blender against the same settings folder. Both runs wrote all eight frames and exited zero. Every install and every removal exited zero. Every frame was then compared picture against picture, and **not one pixel differed in any of the eight frames**. Nothing appeared in the disturbed run's output that was not in the control run's — and both renders were started from the command line, `blender --background scene.blend --render-output <folder>/f_ --render-anim`, which is the route that prints most (settled under **Reports 9**), so the comparison of output carries its full weight.
- **The interface lock with no window.** With no window, the scene was rendered three times on the Linux machine alone, in all four versions — a plain control, one with the lock off and a mid-render install, one with the lock **on** and the same install — and the lock setting was printed back before the assignment, after it, at every one of the eight frames **from inside the render handler**, straight after the install, and after the render. With the lock genuinely on, the install returned `{'FINISHED'}`, all eight frames were written, and every frame matched the control picture for picture; sixty-four such comparisons were made across the four versions, every one of them showing no differing pixel. 5.3 ran its no-window install with the lock off only.
- **A Blender that is already running does not pick the add-on up by itself.** Its switched-on collection stayed empty while a second Blender installed into the same settings folder, and a rescan — `bpy.ops.extensions.repo_refresh_all()`, which returned `{'FINISHED'}` — did not change that. But the add-on could then be switched on inside that same running Blender without restarting it. On Linux the new files on disk were visible to the running Blender straight away.
- **The repository lock is real, and it does stop a second Blender.** This was asked in three parts so that an untroubled install could not be mistaken for a lock that does nothing — one of the three, the lock taken twice in one Blender, is below, because 5.3 ran it too. With one Blender holding it, an install fired from another **refuses in under a second, exit status 1**, naming the session that holds the lock and pointing at "Force Unlock Repository"; the very same command with the lock free installs and exits zero. It does not wait — it refuses.
- **The tested render did not hold that lock.** A second Blender took the lock during an animation render and got `{'FINISHED'}`. This shows that the lock was available during that render. It does not establish why the installs left the frames unchanged, or whether another render or add-on might use the lock.

**The same in all five.**

- **What an install actually touches.** Two things outside the extension's own folder: `extensions/.cache/compat.dat` and `config/userpref.blend`. Adding `--no-prefs` spares the preferences file — and then leaves the add-on **not switched on**, although `-e` was given. On both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, and on the Mac in 5.3. This is the one place the tests say what an install writes, which is the thing no version's sources ever list.
- **Taking the repository lock twice in one Blender says so plainly**, naming the session that already holds it, and the lock appears on disk where there was none — in 5.3, an `index.lock` file inside each repository's `.blender_ext` folder, gone after the unlock. In 5.3 the one second call printed both forms of the message, `Error locking repository "…"` and `Error: Repository "…"`.

**What a program outside the render can see.** With no window, neither `bpy.context.window_manager.is_interface_locked` nor `bpy.app.is_job_running('RENDER')` tells you anything: both read `False` at every moment — in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux, **including with the scene's own lock set on and the render under way**, and in 5.3 on the Mac with the lock off — and `is_job_running` read `False` even from inside a `render_post` handler with the render still going. **With a window open, in 5.3, both work**: `is_job_running('RENDER')` read `True` while the render ran, and `is_interface_locked` read `True` with the lock on and `False` with it off. 5.2.2 on the same Mac, with a window, also read `is_job_running` as `True`. Neither was read with a window in 4.5 to 5.2.0.

**Verdict: not established for every version.** Reading A with no window is the same in all five. Reading B is the same in 4.5 to 5.2, on both machines, and was not tried in 5.3. The windowed frame-handler install is not settled: the earlier 5.2.0 run and the later 5.2.2/5.3 runs differ, and the earlier render-start method is unrecorded. The main-loop install with the lock read back is settled for 5.3 on this Mac alone.

**Where the machines differ.** They do not, in 4.5 to 5.2. What differs is only the paths printed, and how many install-and-remove pairs fitted inside the render — six, one every twelve seconds, on Linux; four or five fired back to back on the Mac for as long as the render lasted. So the Mac's disturbance was the heavier of the two, not the lighter. **No time from any machine is a measurement**: the machines were running other work throughout, so nothing here says how long any render took.

**A note, marked clearly as not established.** On the Mac, of the five installs fired during the Blender 4.5 render, one printed no `STATUS Installed` line at all, where the other four did; it exited zero all the same and the add-on had been installed, as the removal that followed it showed. One occurrence, one machine, one version, and what it means is not settled. **It is not evidence that 4.5 differs from the others**, and it is in no list. It is recorded here so that nobody meets it cold.

**Not established, in any version.** Anything on Windows. **Where the install wrote:** every install in these tests, in every version, went into a **user** repository inside a settings folder of its own, and nothing was ever written into the folder a running Blender was itself running from — which is where a Blender's own System repository sits by default, and which is the one kind of write **Builds 6** records as killing a render on Linux. And whether an install is harmless in general, rather than at the rate and in the manner these tests fired it — what the tests show is that these installs, fired this often, changed no frame. **Not established for 4.5 to 5.2:** a windowed render with the lock read back. **Not established for 5.3:** reading B in every part.

**A free mitigation for the untried case, for a machine being built.** Pointing `BLENDER_SYSTEM_EXTENSIONS` at a folder outside every Blender's own folder moves the System repository out of the folder a Blender runs from. That is the arrangement question 14's system-extensions test was run under, across 4.5 to 5.2 and in 5.3 alone.

**One thing bearing on this that the sources leave open.** When preferences are saved is not settled in 5.0's sources: both "saved on exit" and "automatically saved when changed" are stated. This bears on the question because an install writes the preferences file. 5.3's sources say they are saved on exit when changed, and do not say whether that happens with no window; in 5.3, on the Mac, a script's change made with no window was not saved on exit (question 3).

## 13. Once an add-on is installed and switched on, what more can be learned about whether it will work?

**How it is known.** Documented in every version, and all five mean the same thing by it: the sources give no single verdict on whether an add-on will work, only a list of places where trouble shows. Every version's list is built from the same handful of manual pages and says the same things — read the console; rescan and see the warnings; open the add-on's own box; remember that its own preferences may all be off; check that a bundled module really loaded; check where it landed; check that a compiled part suits the machine; check whether it is allowed on the internet; list what is installed; validate the manifest. Hand tests were run in 4.5 to 5.2 on the RTX 4080 SUPER machine — the fourth rented Linux machine — and again on the Creator's Mac; one, which needs a screen, was run on the Mac alone. 5.3 was run on the Mac, with no window and with one.

**Four things behave identically in every version** — `bpy.ops.preferences.addon_refresh()`, `bpy.app.is_job_running` with no window, `bpy.utils.extension_path_user` and the workspace add-on filter — so that where only one version's sources name one of them, the others' silence is not a change in Blender. Two things that looked the same way turned out to be real differences: the readable reports list and the logging arguments, both below.

**The same in all five — on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, on the Mac in 5.3.**

- **Switching an add-on on does give a machine-readable signal, which no version's sources describe.** With a working extension and three broken in different ways — one whose `register()` raises, one importing a module that is not there, one whose operator declares a number where a name belongs — the operator `bpy.ops.preferences.addon_enable` returns `{'FINISHED'}` for the working one and **raises** for each broken one, with a message naming the fault. After the operator, both `bpy.context.preferences.addons` and `addon_utils.check` include the working one and exclude every broken one. `addon_utils.enable` returns the module for the working one and `None` for each broken one, swallowing the error rather than raising; in 5.3, after `addon_utils.enable` with its defaults, the working add-on was loaded but was **not** in the switched-on collection, which 4.5 to 5.2 did not record either way.
- **Where the messages go.** The full traceback goes to the error stream. The ordinary output carries an `Error:` line naming the fault — and, where the add-on's own `register()` raised, an `Exception in module register(): <path>` line before it. That second line was seen in 5.3 and in 5.2.2 on the same Mac the same day; the 4.5 to 5.2.0 records quote the `Error:` line only. Through `addon_utils.enable`, in 5.3, the ordinary output carried the `Exception in module register()` line and no `Error:` line.
- **But that collection records what was asked for, not what loaded.** An add-on that was switched on and then broke on disk **stays in the collection** after a restart, with its failure printed at start-up. So the lookup can only be trusted about a switch-on made in the same run.
- **The rescan reports nothing about damage to the code.** Against three kinds of damage in turn — text that is not Python in `__init__.py`, a deleted manifest, a deleted `__init__.py` — `bpy.ops.extensions.repo_refresh_all()` returned `{'FINISHED'}`. In the earlier runs each fault surfaced at start-up before that call, which named no damaged add-on. With the manifest deleted, the extension disappears from the package listing while still loading and registering. In 5.3 and in the same-day 5.2.2 control, a run called **both** `repo_refresh_all()` and `bpy.ops.preferences.addon_refresh()`: it printed the missing-manifest warning before the first returned and again before the second returned. This does not establish that either call alone printed it twice. With the other two kinds of damage neither call named the damaged add-on. `repo_refresh_all()` also warned that the official repository had no data on disk, which is about the repository and not the add-on. The earlier excerpts do not establish whether they omitted these warnings or behaved differently.
- **The listing says nothing about health, and nothing about on or off.** A working switched-on extension, one installed and left off, and one whose switch-on had failed all print the same `[installed]` line. With no internet allowed, `extension list -s` refuses outright and exits 1.
- **`validate` can be pointed at an installed extension, and only parses the manifest.** It accepts an installed folder and prints `Success parsing TOML`, exit status 0 — including for an add-on that cannot be imported at all, and for one whose code has been replaced with text that is not Python. It refuses a folder with no manifest, and one with no `__init__.py`. So it is a manifest check and not a health check, in every version.
- **Exit status.** A failed switch-on leaves the exit status at zero unless `--python-exit-code` is given **and** the error is left uncaught, in which case it becomes 1 and Blender prints that the script failed. The same failure through `addon_utils.enable` exits zero either way. Naming a broken add-on at launch with `--addons` prints its traceback and still exits zero.
- **Seventeen checks were asked of a Blender with no window.** Sixteen answered in every version: the add-ons collection, `addon_utils.paths()`, `bpy.app.background`, `bpy.app.online_access`, existence checks for both spellings of the override property, `bpy.app.is_job_running('RENDER')`, `bpy.utils.extension_path_user`, the workspace filter property, `bpy.ops.extensions.repo_refresh_all()`, `bpy.ops.preferences.addon_refresh()`, `bpy.ops.preferences.addon_expand()`, `bpy.ops.extensions.status_clear_errors()`, the enable operator's argument names, the add-on's own preferences and the preferences version all returned rather than raising. The seventeenth is the reports list, and it answers in 5.2 and 5.3 only — the version difference below. **These tested checks are reachable with no window**, subject to that version limit. **Reachable is not the same as useful:** `bpy.app.is_job_running('RENDER')` answers when it is asked and, with no window, still does not tell you a render is running — under question 12.
- **A legacy add-on's declared `warning` reads back from Python exactly as written.** In 4.5 to 5.2 it came back alongside five keys Blender fills in that the file never carried; in 5.3, read straight off the module, it came back with nothing added. The earlier reading route is not recorded. Whether the added-key difference comes from that route, the build or the version is not established; it is not an established version difference.
- **On screen, on the Mac, in all five versions.** A declared warning **is** shown: the row carries a warning triangle where other rows carry the plain icon, and opening the row shows a `Warning` line with the text in it. An add-on that **failed to switch on is not marked at all** — its row is indistinguishable from one simply left switched off, and opening it shows only the ordinary lines. And an add-on declaring it needs a Blender far newer than the one running is in the list and can be switched on, which agrees with what the Python side reported. All five versions look the same.

**Verdict: it differs**, on two points, both established by tests run in every version on the Mac, and in 4.5 to 5.2 on Linux as well.

**Blender 4.5.** `bpy.context.window_manager.reports` does not exist — reading it raises `AttributeError: 'WindowManager' object has no attribute 'reports'`. `--log-list-categories` is refused: it is read as a file name, `unknown argument, loading as file: --log-list-categories`, then `Error: Cannot read file`, exit status 1. The help text carries `--log-show-basename` and `--log-show-timestamp`.

**Blender 5.0 and 5.1.** `bpy.context.window_manager.reports` does not exist — reading it raises. `--log-list-categories` is accepted and prints a list of categories beginning `addon`, `anim`, `anim.action`. The help text carries `--log-show-memory` and `--log-show-source`.

**Blender 5.2.** `bpy.context.window_manager.reports` **does** exist and returns a list, empty in a fresh run; 5.2's release notes state the addition outright — Blender's reports are now a read-only list on window-manager data, each carrying a message, a severity and a session-wide identifier. So anything that wants to read Blender's own warnings and errors back from a script has that route from 5.2 on. `--log-list-categories` is accepted. The help text carries `--log-show-memory` and `--log-show-source`, and gives `--factory-startup` the short form `-X`.

**Blender 5.3.** `bpy.context.window_manager.reports` exists and returns a list, empty in a fresh run, as in 5.2. `--log-list-categories` is accepted and prints a list beginning `addon`, `anim`, `anim.action`, `anim.data`. The help text carries `--log-show-memory` and `--log-show-source`; whether it gives `--factory-startup` the short form `-X` was not recorded.

Everything else looked for is in 4.5 to 5.2 — `--log`, `--log-level`, `--log-file`, `--log-show-backtrace`, `--addons`, `--python-exit-code`, `--offline-mode`, `--online-mode`, `--factory-startup`, `--env-system-scripts`, `--env-system-extensions` and `-c/--command`. In 5.3 all of these were seen in the help text or used in the tests except `--env-system-scripts` and `--env-system-extensions`, whose presence is not established. `--log-level 2` is accepted by 4.5 to 5.2, but only 5.0, 5.1 and 5.2 print anything extra at that level, in a timestamped form; 5.3 was not asked. 5.0's release notes say the logging system changed in that version, which agrees with what was seen. Results without a matching test in another version remain unestablished there.

**Not established, in any version.** What the extensions error status actually holds. `bpy.ops.extensions.status_clear_errors()` returns `{'FINISHED'}` and prints nothing, in every version, and no version's documentation describes the status at all — even the step from the operator's *name* to "an error status exists" is an inference. Also not established, in any version: whether any of this behaves the same on Windows. And nothing anywhere, documented or tested, tells you before an add-on is used whether the part of it you actually need will work — every check named here is a check that something has already gone wrong.

## 14. Does an add-on set up for one Blender work in another Blender on the same computer, and what decides that?

**How it is known.** The mechanism is documented in every version: each version's configuration files are stored in separate folders. That an add-on installed under one version is not seen by another is an inference in some versions' sources, 5.3's included, and taken straight from that sentence in others. The observations in 4.5 to 5.2 come from the RTX 4080 SUPER Linux machine and the Creator's Mac; cases run on one only are named below. **In 5.3, on the Mac, only what one Blender can show was tested**: the Python it carries, a system-extensions folder, a system scripts folder, one extensions folder shared by two settings folders, declared version ranges, a legacy minimum, and whether the copy-from-previous-version operator can run. **Nothing in 5.3 crossed to another version, and a second copy of 5.3 could not be started.**

**The same in 4.5 to 5.2, on both machines.**

- **Nothing is shared between versions by default.** Four differently-named extensions were installed, one by each Blender, into one shared parent folder. Each Blender listed only its own, and four separate version-named folders sat on disk. So the step every version's sources leave to the reader is right, and it is observed rather than reasoned. For 5.3 it rests on its sources' inference.
- **The folder each Blender reads is named for two numbers, never three.** Blender 4.5.12 reads a folder called `4.5`; the patch number is in `bpy.app.version` and never enters the path. Starting Blender does not create the folder — only an install or a saved preference does. The one time 5.3 wrote into its default user folder, by accident, that folder was `…/Blender/5.3/` (question 6); and in 5.3 reading and listing wrote nothing into a settings folder, whose `config` folder appeared only with an install that switched an add-on on or a saved preference.
- **Five separate ways of sharing all work, across 4.5 to 5.2.** One extensions folder handed to all four makes the add-on visible and loadable to all four, though only the version that did the installing has it switched on in its own collection. One settings folder handed to all four makes it visible, loadable **and** switched on for all four. One system-extensions folder serves all four, listed under System. One scripts folder serves all four, for an older-style add-on. And an installed folder simply copied by hand from one version's folder into another's works too, in both directions — out of 4.5 into each of the other three, and out of 5.2 into each of the other three. **So nothing in Blender 4.5 to 5.2 refuses an add-on because another version installed it**; it is the default folder's version-numbered name that keeps versions apart, and nothing else. **All five sharing tests used a plain-Python add-on with no bundled module.** A bundled module is unpacked into a folder named for the running Blender's Python — `python3.11` for 4.5 and 5.0, `python3.13` from 5.1 on — and that folder sits under the settings folder being shared, and nothing goes back to unpack another version's copy (question 4). So whether a shared folder serves an add-on with a compiled part across the 5.0-to-5.1 Python line is **untried in every version**, and the install-time check that would catch it cannot fire, because the second version installs nothing.
- **Two copies of one build share, and a `portable` folder takes over completely.** A second copy of a Blender computed the same user folder as the original and listed what the original had installed. A `portable` folder beside the copy moved it entirely to its own — on the Mac that folder goes inside the bundle at `Contents/Resources`, exactly where the sources place it. `BLENDER_USER_RESOURCES` still wins over a `portable` folder. **In 5.3 this was not settled**: a copy of 5.3 made on the Mac never started, because macOS held it before any of Blender's own code ran.

**What 5.3 showed from one Blender, on the Mac.** The three sharing routes that need only one version work in 5.3 within itself. An extension placed in a folder named by `BLENDER_SYSTEM_EXTENSIONS` is listed under System and switches on as `bl_ext.system.<id>`. An older-style add-on in a scripts folder named by `BLENDER_SYSTEM_SCRIPTS` is found and switches on, and Blender's own add-ons are still all there with that variable set. And one extensions folder named by `BLENDER_USER_EXTENSIONS` and shared by two settings folders makes an extension installed through the first visible and loadable through the second, but switched on at start-up only in the settings folder whose preferences record it — the same shape 4.5 to 5.2 gave across versions.

**The same in all five — on both machines in 4.5 to 5.2, on the Mac in 5.3.**

- **The tested extension version ranges are enforced at install.** An extension declaring it needs at least Blender 99, and one declaring it does not support 4.3 and above, were both built and validated without complaint and then **refused at install** by every version, with a message naming both the running Blender and the declared limit. Nothing was installed, nothing reached the listing, and nothing could be switched on. **The install command still exits zero.** Anything relying on the exit status to know whether an install worked will be told nothing.
- **The tested legacy minimum does not hide or block the add-on, contrary to the manual.** Every version's manual says an older Blender "won't display the add-on in the list". A one-file add-on declaring it needs Blender 99 was listed alongside an ordinary one by every Blender, read back with its declaration intact, switched on by `addon_utils.enable` with no message, and had its `register()` run. On screen, on the Mac, its row is in the list and its tick box can be ticked, in all five. In 5.3 the preferences operator switched it on too, printing a warning as it did so — that it was written for Blender 99.0.0 and "might not function (correctly), though it is enabled". What the operator prints for it in 4.5 to 5.2 was not recorded.
- **The tested bundled package naming another Python is not taken into use.** The install prints an error naming both Pythons and exits 1. Where the package matches, it installs and the bundled module can be imported from a shared place under the settings folder — `python3.11` for 4.5 and 5.0, `python3.13` from 5.1 on. In 5.3, and in 5.2.2 on the same Mac the same day, the error follows a `STATUS Installed` line, and the add-on's folder, its bundled file unopened, then stays on disk and is listed as installed — but it is not switched on, nothing is unpacked, and its module will not import. The 4.5 to 5.2.0 records quote the error line only, so whether those versions leave the folder too was not recorded. The rejection is observed at install. In 5.3 the wheels were hand-made and contained no compiled code, so that test establishes rejection by the declared Python name, not execution of a compiled part.

**The tests establish four factors:** which folders Blender reads; the declared Blender version range, which refused the tested extensions but did not hide or block the tested legacy add-on; the Python it carries, for bundled modules; and the kind of computer the package names. All four were observed in 4.5 to 5.2. 5.3 tested the last three from one Blender, using hand-made wheels without compiled code; it also read the folders it was pointed at, never beside another version. **This is not a complete test of compatibility.** The version-number guidance also documents that changes to Blender's Python interface or features can break an add-on. That factor was not tested here.

**Verdict: it differs.** Every version was answered and the answers agree at every point but two; 5.3's answer on crossing between versions rests on its sources' inference, not on a test.

**Blender 4.5 and 5.0.** Python 3.11 — 3.11.11 and 3.11.13 respectively — so a bundled module must be a `cp311` file and lands under `python3.11`. Read in 4.5 to 5.2 on both machines, and the Mac builds carry the same four as the Linux builds. Even the two versions on 3.11 are not on the same 3.11.

**Blender 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3.** Python 3.13 — 3.13.9 in 5.1, and 3.13.13 in both 5.2 and 5.3 — so a bundled module must be a `cp313` file and lands under `python3.13`. 5.1's release notes state the Python upgrade outright. 5.3 was read on the Mac, and carries exactly the Python 5.2 does.

The second difference is the wording of the second line printed when an extension is refused for its declared Blender version and `-e` was given: `Package should have been installed but not found: <id>` in 4.5 and 5.0, `Package was expected to be installed but not found: <id>` in 5.1 to 5.3. It is word for word the same split recorded under question 4 for a refusal on the wrong kind of computer, and the first line, naming both versions, is the same shape in all five.

**`copy_prev()` — a difference between the machines, and not between the versions.** The one route the sources give for carrying an older version's add-ons into a newer one is `bpy.ops.preferences.copy_prev()`, which every version's sources describe only as something done at the splash screen. On the Linux machine it ran with no window, in 4.5 to 5.2, with the version folders sitting in Blender's own layout under a stand-in home folder: 5.0 picked up from 4.5, 5.1 from 5.0 and 5.2 from 5.1, each returning `{'FINISHED'}`, each ending with the older version's installed extension in its own folder and its own listing. **4.5 refused there, but 4.5 had no earlier version on that machine to copy from, so its refusal says nothing about Blender 4.5.** With the settings folder handed over by `BLENDER_USER_RESOURCES` instead, **all four refused on Linux** and nothing crossed. On the Creator's Mac the operator was unavailable in **all five** versions. In 5.3, with the settings folder handed over, calling it with no window raised `poll() failed, context is incorrect`; with a window open, its `poll()` returned `False`, and it was not called. In 4.5 to 5.2 it refused with a window open and with the Creator's own earlier-version folders in place; 5.3 was never pointed at those folders. On these machines, availability differs with the operating system and folder arrangement. No version difference is established. This machine result belongs in no version-difference list.

**Two more things that are about macOS rather than about any version.** A stand-in home folder does not move Blender's user folder on macOS, though `BLENDER_USER_RESOURCES` does; on Linux either works. Because of that, every Mac test needing the four versions to sit under one parent had that parent laid out by hand, so those tests show what each version does with the folder it is given rather than that Blender chose the folders — and the folder Blender computes for itself was confirmed separately, on both machines, in 4.5 to 5.2. Second: on the Mac the `portable` folder goes inside the application bundle, where the sources place it. Neither is a difference between versions.

**One reading is not clean, and the point does not rest on it.** In the Linux runs of the bundled-package test, the import step for 4.5 and 5.0 was contaminated by what the previous case had left in a shared folder. The Mac runs gave every case a settings folder of its own, in 4.5 to 5.2 and again in 5.3, which removes the contamination, and their readings agree with the refusal messages. So the point stands on the Mac's evidence, and the Linux refusal messages and exit statuses — which were never in doubt — agree with it.

**Not established, in any version.** Two different builds of one major-and-minor version sharing a folder — it was not posed here, and the nearest thing posed anywhere is **Builds 5**'s pair of 5.2.0 builds from two sources, which **Builds 3** finds identical in hash, build date and build options, so they may be one build delivered two ways; two Blenders built for different operating systems on one computer; and anything on Windows, which leaves the Windows paths and the Windows `portable` placement untested. All three are silences in what could be tried rather than differences between versions. **Not established for 5.3:** every crossing between 5.3 and another version; two copies of 5.3 and a `portable` folder; and which folder 5.3 computes with nothing overridden.
